Defining the target/prediction task precisely.

Top-gainer lists are cross-sectional rankings computed at time T; the tradeable version is: at close T (or some cutoff), predict forward return over horizon h, for the cross-section, and hold the top-k.
Need to define: prediction target (h-bar forward return, or direction, or probability of exceeding a hurdle = TP level, or triple-barrier label).
TP/SL: don't bolt them on as an afterthought. They define the label AND the exit policy. Use triple-barrier labeling (López de Prado): upper barrier = TP in vol units, lower = SL, vertical barrier = time stop. This makes the label match the actual trade. Then meta-labeling: primary model picks direction, secondary model says "is this signal worth taking" and sizes.
Key: TP/SL must be volatility-scaled (ATR multiples or sigma), not fixed percents, otherwise you're just measuring vol.

Study design for the "which is more predictable" question — this is the core.

Hypothesis space is a hierarchy: asset class → symbol → symbol×direction → symbol×direction×regime/horizon. Danger: multiple testing explodes. With 5000 stocks × 2 directions × 5 horizons = 50,000 tests; at α=0.05 you get 2,500 false positives.
Approach: nested/hierarchical modeling with shrinkage (partial pooling / empirical Bayes / hierarchical Bayesian). Estimate effect per symbol but shrink toward asset-class and global mean. Then measure how much of the variance in edge is explained by each level — that directly answers "is it the asset class, the symbol, or the direction that matters?" Use variance decomposition (ICC) / ANOVA-style. This is the elegant answer.
Also: pre-register the hypothesis. Split sample: train / validation / holdout (never touch). Walk-forward with purging and embargo (López de Prado) to prevent leakage from overlapping labels.
Statistical quantification of evidence: deflated Sharpe ratio, probability of backtest overfitting (PBO / CSCV), White's Reality Check / Hansen's SPA, Romano-Wolf stepdown for multiple testing. These are "what is proven" — the honest answer: nothing is ever proven, you can only bound the probability that your result came from noise.
Also block/bootstrap with regime awareness, and "minimum backtest length" — how long a history you'd need to distinguish a Sharpe of X from 0 at a given confidence.

Baselines and null models — mandatory.

Buy and hold, equal-weight, random-entry with same holding period and same vol, momentum, reversal, cross-sectional 12-1 momentum, short-term reversal. If your model doesn't beat "random entry with the same exposure profile", you've found nothing.
Crucially: top-gainers strategies have huge implicit exposure to momentum and to beta/high-beta/small-cap/illiquid factors. Attribute returns to common factors before claiming skill (Fama-French/Carhart, crypto factors: market, size, momentum, liquidity).

Backtest mechanics.

Event-driven simulator, bar-by-bar; orders fill at next bar open or with pessimistic assumptions; partial fills; slippage model calibrated to your size; bar-level vs intrabar path ambiguity (if both TP and SL are hit in the same bar, resolve pessimistically — this is a classic TP/SL backtest inflation bug).
Costs: implementation shortfall. Crypto: taker fees + funding + slippage on thin books. FX: spread + swap/rollover. Stocks: spread + commission + borrow for shorts + hard-to-borrow.
Capacity: your edge per trade × trade size vs ADV. Top gainers in microcaps are capacity-tiny.
Regime: split results by VIX/vol regime, trend vs chop; a strategy that only works in 2020 or 2021 is a bet on a regime.
Survivorship/delisting handling: crypto pair delisting happens; backtest must include the death of coins.
Also test execution at realistic times: many "top gainer" effects exist only in the pre/after-hours or in the first 5 minutes.

Forward testing (paper/live shadow).

Deploy the exact frozen pipeline with the exact same code path as backtest (parity), record every signal before outcome (timestamped, append-only log, hashed) — this is your proof of no hindsight.
Start with a pre-registered signal schedule; track forecast vs realized, compare live metric distribution to backtest distribution using a proper test (e.g., a sequential test / SPRT, or CUSUM to detect drift).
Statistical power: how long must you run? Compute it upfront — e.g., to detect Sharpe 1.0 annualized with 95% confidence you need roughly (t/Sharpe)^2 → 2-5 years; so instead measure per-trade edge with many trades, dozens-hundreds of trades minimum. Be honest: forward tests short of ~100+ independent trades prove almost nothing; use it for detecting operational/data bugs and drift, not for confirming edge.
Kill criteria and drift monitoring pre-registered. Champion/challenger. Monitor feature distributions (PSI), label distribution, edge by cohort.

Metrics — organize into tiers.

Predictive/discrimination: hit rate, AUC/ROC, precision@k (top-k of ranking), rank IC / Spearman correlation between predicted and realized (this is the single best metric for cross-sectional "top gainers" problems), information coefficient decay by horizon, calibration (Brier score, reliability curve) for probabilities.
Economic per trade: expectancy in R units (R = risk per trade = distance to SL), average win/loss, payoff ratio, profit factor, expectancy per trade with confidence interval, PSR/deflated Sharpe, Sortino, MaxDD, recovery, time in market, exposure.
Robustness: parameter sensitivity surface (edge should be a plateau not a spike), bootstrap CI, subperiod/SYMBOL-level dispersion (does one symbol or one week drive all the profit?), leave-one-out by symbol, regime breakdown, capacity.
Attribution: alpha vs factors, beta, correlation to existing book.
Operational: fill rate, slippage vs model, latency, downtime.

"What is proven" — be honest. Academic record:

Time-series momentum (Moskowitz Ooi Pedersen), cross-sectional momentum (Jegadeesh-Titman), short-term reversal in equities, value, low-vol. Crypto: momentum and size/liquidity effects documented but weak/decaying. FX: carry and momentum/trend are the two robust ones.

# If you were tasked to reverse engineer the daily or weekly top gainers for stocks ,crypto mutual funds etc, how would you go about ? i.e. you want to be able to give signals including a TP/SL for stocks/crypto, maybe forex, and determine first of all which asset class is  more predictable.. is a particular "symbol" more predictable, or a symbol and a direction like long vs short, ? what is "proven" , would you back-test, and how can you forward-test, and which metrics would you need to measure

## ChatGPT-6 Luna

I’d treat this as a **research problem about tradable, out-of-sample predictability**—not just a project to reproduce historical “top gainers” lists. A model that identifies assets that already rose is easy to build; the hard part is showing that its signals can predict *future* returns after costs and risk.

## 1. Define exactly what you want to predict

“Top gainer” needs a precise definition:

- **Horizon:** next day, next week, or another period?
- **Universe:** which exchanges, funds, crypto assets, or currency pairs?
- **Return:** close-to-close, open-to-close, or return from a realistically executable entry?
- **Target:** highest absolute return, highest risk-adjusted return, or best return relative to a benchmark?
- **Trading direction:** long only, short only, or either?

These are different prediction tasks. Predicting the next week’s top 10 gainers is a **cross-sectional ranking** problem. Predicting whether one symbol will rise over the next week is a **time-series direction** problem. You may want to test both.

For a top-gainers strategy, define the target using future returns—for example, whether an asset will rank in the top 5% of its eligible universe over the *next* five trading days. Do not define the target using information that would only be known after the trade could have been entered.

## 2. Build a trustworthy dataset

Historical data problems can make a weak strategy appear strong. I would make sure the data includes:

- **Point-in-time universes:** include assets that later delisted, failed, or became illiquid. Otherwise survivorship bias favors today’s winners.
- **Correct corporate-action adjustments:** splits, distributions, symbol changes, and similar events for stocks and funds.
- **Tradable prices:** use prices consistent with when a signal could actually have been entered—not just an idealized closing price.
- **Realistic costs:** commissions, bid–ask spread, slippage, borrow costs for shorts, funding for perpetual crypto, and market impact where relevant.
- **Liquidity and trading restrictions:** exclude or constrain assets that could not realistically be traded at the intended size.
- **Consistent timestamps:** crypto trades 24/7; stocks, funds, and forex have different trading hours and data conventions.

A “top gainer” list is particularly vulnerable to low-liquidity spikes, stale prices, price limits, and bad data. Those need explicit handling.

## 3. Compare asset classes fairly

I would not assume that one asset class is inherently more predictable. Predictability depends on the **horizon, universe, trading costs, market regime, and strategy**. For example, a signal may work for liquid large-cap stocks over a week but fail for small caps, or work in one crypto regime and disappear in another.

To compare asset classes, use the same broad research standards:

- Evaluate out-of-sample performance after realistic costs.
- Compare returns at similar risk levels, or use risk-adjusted results.
- Include realistic trading frequency and liquidity constraints.
- Report the results by market regime and over multiple time periods.

There are also important differences: mutual funds generally have once-daily NAVs and may not support short-term exits; forex lacks a single consolidated exchange tape; crypto has continuous trading and exchange-specific liquidity and funding. A common “daily” test must account for those differences.

## 4. Test both symbol and direction

“Is this symbol predictable?” is usually too broad. A more useful question is:

> **For this symbol, in this direction, over this horizon, under these conditions, is the expected net return positive and sufficiently reliable?**

Long and short behavior can differ substantially. A model may have evidence for long signals but none for shorts, or vice versa. It may also only work in specific volatility or trend regimes.

I’d test:

1. **Cross-sectional ranking:** which assets are likely to outperform others?
2. **Directional prediction:** is a particular asset more likely to rise or fall?
3. **Conditional performance:** does the signal behave differently by volatility, trend, liquidity, or broader market conditions?

The model should ideally produce more than “up” or “down”: an estimated probability, expected return, uncertainty, and the conditions under which the signal is valid.

## 5. Backtest without leaking the future

A sensible research sequence is:

1. Create simple baselines: buy-and-hold, market/sector benchmark, momentum, and random or naïve selection.
2. Define features using only information available at the signal time. Candidate features might include past returns, volatility, volume, spreads, trend, relative strength, and market-wide conditions.
3. Split the data chronologically. Use **walk-forward testing**: train on earlier data, test on the next period, then roll forward.
4. Keep a final period untouched until the research choices are complete.
5. If labels overlap in time, use appropriate purging or embargoes so training examples do not leak information into test periods.
6. Record every experiment. Trying many symbols, indicators, thresholds, and stop settings can produce a convincing result by chance.

A random train/test split is usually unsuitable for market time series. Nor is it enough to find one profitable backtest. Look for similar evidence across multiple untouched periods, markets, and reasonable parameter choices.

## 6. Define TP/SL as part of the tested strategy

Take-profit and stop-loss levels should be tied to the trade’s horizon, volatility, and risk—not selected solely because they maximize historical profit.

One practical method is to define outcomes with **barriers**:

- a profit target,
- a stop level,
- and a maximum holding time.

Then estimate how often each barrier is reached first, and the resulting return after costs. Volatility-scaled distances, such as ATR- or realized-volatility-based levels, are often more comparable across assets than fixed percentage levels.

Be careful with daily OHLC backtests: if both a stop and target fall within the same bar, you may not know which was hit first. Use finer-grained data when possible, or make a conservative assumption. Also account for gaps, spread, slippage, and the fact that a stop price is not necessarily the execution price.

A useful signal might look like:

- symbol and direction,
- signal time and intended entry method,
- holding horizon,
- estimated probability of target versus stop,
- expected net return and uncertainty,
- TP, SL, and time exit,
- position size based on a predefined risk budget.

The TP/SL should be evaluated together with the entry rule. A good directional forecast can still be a poor trading strategy if the exits, costs, or sizing are bad.

## 7. Measure more than hit rate

A high percentage of winning trades does not necessarily mean a profitable strategy. I would measure at least:

**Prediction and ranking**
- Precision/recall for the selected top-gainer group
- Precision@K and rank correlation between predicted and realized returns
- Directional accuracy, compared with a relevant baseline
- Probability calibration: when the model says “60%,” does that outcome occur about 60% of the time?

**Trading performance**
- Net return, CAGR or annualized return, and benchmark-relative return
- Sharpe and Sortino ratios
- Maximum drawdown and time to recovery
- Win rate, average win, average loss, expectancy, and profit factor
- Turnover, exposure, leverage, and capacity
- Results after fees, spread, slippage, borrow/funding, and other costs

**Robustness**
- Results by year, market regime, symbol group, and liquidity level
- Sensitivity to reasonable parameter changes
- Confidence intervals, preferably using methods that account for serial dependence
- Performance of the worst periods, not just the average

For a TP/SL strategy, also track the distribution of realized outcomes, stop gaps, time exits, and how often each barrier is reached.

## 8. Forward-test before relying on it

After research backtests, run the strategy in **paper trading** with live or near-live data. Log each signal at the time it occurs, including the quoted entry, intended size, TP/SL, and the price actually available. Do not revise past signals after seeing what happened.

Paper trading tests data, timing, order logic, and operational assumptions, but simulated fills can still be optimistic. If it remains promising, a small, tightly risk-limited live test can reveal real execution costs and slippage. Set the evaluation period and success criteria in advance; do not stop the test merely because a short stretch looks good or bad.

## What would count as “proven”?

Nothing in markets is proven permanently. A strategy has **credible evidence of an edge** when it:

- works on data not used to develop it,
- beats sensible benchmarks after realistic costs,
- remains reasonably robust across time and implementation choices,
- has acceptable drawdowns and capacity,
- and continues to behave consistently in forward testing.

The central result is not “this ticker is predictable.” It is something narrower and testable, such as: **“Under these conditions, this rule has had a positive net expected return over this horizon, with this level of uncertainty and drawdown.”**

## Claude (Latest Model)

Here's how I'd approach it, in the order I'd actually do the work.

## 1. Define the target precisely

"Top gainers" is an **ex-post** label: you only know who topped the list after the move. You can't trade the label directly, so the real problem is predicting it from information available *before* the move. Specify:

- **Universe:** e.g., US stocks above $X market cap and $Y average daily dollar volume, or top-100 crypto by volume.
- **Horizon:** intraday, next day, next week.
- **Label:** e.g., "top decile of next-day return," or better, a path-based label (see step 4).
- **Trade definition:** entry time, direction, stop, target, max holding time, position sizing.

Predicting "will be a top gainer" and predicting "will hit +5% before -3% within 3 days" are different problems. Pick one and be explicit.

## 2. Data you need

- Daily and intraday OHLCV, adjusted for splits and dividends.
- **Survivorship-free** universes, including delisted names. Without this, backtests look far better than reality.
- Point-in-time fundamentals and float, so you don't use data that wasn't known yet.
- Timestamped news and earnings dates.
- For crypto: funding rates, open interest, exchange-specific listings and delistings.
- For forex: spreads, swap/rollover costs, session times.
- Realistic cost model: commissions, spreads, slippage that scales with volume, borrow fees for shorts, funding for perps.

## 3. Descriptive study first (event study)

Before building any model, look at what happened *before* past gainers moved:

- Relative volume spikes, gap size, prior returns, volatility compression, sector/market beta, market cap, news or earnings timing.
- Compare against matched non-gainers with similar characteristics to separate real signal from base-rate noise.
- Check whether the pattern is a **continuation** (momentum/drift) or a **fade** (reversal). This depends heavily on horizon. Very short-term returns often mean-revert, while medium-term (weeks to months) momentum has historically persisted. Post-earnings drift and gap-and-go behavior are also documented.

## 4. Build the signal with a path-based label

For TP/SL trading, don't use "next-day return" as the label. Use the **triple-barrier method** (Lopez de Prado):

- Upper barrier = take-profit, lower barrier = stop-loss, vertical barrier = max holding time.
- Label = which barrier is hit first (+1, -1, or timeout).

This matches how you'd actually trade. It also exposes a key fact: with symmetric TP/SL, a 50% hit rate is breakeven before costs. **Your edge can come from payoff asymmetry or hit rate, but you must measure the combination.** A 35% hit rate at 3:1 reward/risk can be strong, and a 70% hit rate at 1:3 can be a loser.

Model choices: start simple (logistic regression or gradient boosting on a few interpretable features), because complex models overfit financial data very easily.

## 5. Which asset class is more predictable?

I wouldn't assume an answer. My prior, which you should test:

- **Equities:** well-documented anomalies (momentum, post-earnings drift, short-term reversal, low-volatility), but large caps are efficient and small caps are costly to trade. Shorting has borrow costs and squeeze risk.
- **Crypto:** more apparent inefficiency and bigger moves, so more signal, but higher costs, regime shifts, thin liquidity, and manipulation. Edges tend to decay or vanish quickly.
- **Forex:** major pairs are very efficient. Directional prediction is notoriously hard. Carry and trend effects are documented but small relative to costs.

To compare fairly, measure each class on the same metrics, after costs, with the same methodology and comparable trade counts. Raw "accuracy" across classes isn't comparable because volatility and cost structures differ.

## 6. Symbol vs. direction

This is a useful question to frame as a hierarchy:

- **Pooled model first.** Train across many symbols with features that generalize (volatility-normalized returns, relative volume, etc.). This is where most robust signal lives.
- **Direction as a separate question.** Long and short often have different base rates, different costs, and different behavior (shorting gainers after a spike is a different bet from buying them). Model them separately or test them as separate outcomes.
- **Per-symbol models last, and heavily shrunk.** Each symbol has few independent events, so per-symbol "edges" are mostly noise. If you do use them, use hierarchical/Bayesian shrinkage toward the pooled estimate and require enough events per symbol.

Asking "is this symbol predictable?" with a few years of data usually produces false positives.

## 7. Backtesting done properly

- **Walk-forward** evaluation: fit on a window, test on the next, roll forward.
- **Purged, embargoed cross-validation** for overlapping labels, so information from the future doesn't leak into training.
- **No look-ahead:** signals use only data available at the timestamp. Watch for daily bars that close after the decision time, and for corporate actions applied retroactively.
- **Realistic execution:** fill at next bar's open or with slippage, not at the signal price. Include gaps through stops (you don't always exit at your SL; gaps can blow through it).
- **Parameter stability:** if performance collapses when a threshold moves 10%, the strategy is curve-fit.
- **Log every trial.** Every parameter sweep and model variant counts toward multiple testing. Keep a record of how many things you tried.

## 8. Guarding against false discovery

With enough variants, something will look profitable by chance. Use:

- **Deflated Sharpe Ratio**, which adjusts for the number of trials and non-normal returns.
- **White's Reality Check** or **Hansen's SPA test** for data-snooping across strategies.
- **Permutation/Monte Carlo tests:** shuffle signals or shift them in time and see how often random signals match your results.
- **Bootstrap confidence intervals** on expectancy and Sharpe. If the interval includes zero, you don't have an edge.
- **Holdout set** you touch once, at the end.

## 9. Forward testing

- **Paper trade live** with signals timestamped and frozen before the outcome is known. Don't backfill.
- Run in **shadow mode** against real market data, including realistic fills and spreads.
- Track **live vs. backtest divergence**: hit rate, average R, slippage, trade frequency. Large gaps signal either bad execution assumptions or a broken edge.
- Set **kill criteria in advance**, such as "stop if drawdown exceeds X or expectancy is negative over N trades," so you don't rationalize a failing system.
- Forward testing needs real time. A few weeks rarely tells you much for low-frequency strategies. Plan for months.

## 10. Metrics to track

| Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Hit rate (win %) | Fraction of trades hitting TP before SL |
| Average win / average loss (R-multiple) | Payoff asymmetry |
| **Expectancy** = hit% × avgWin − loss% × avgLoss | Average profit per trade, after costs. The core metric. |
| Profit factor | Gross profit / gross loss |
| Sharpe / Sortino | Risk-adjusted return (Sortino penalizes only downside) |
| Max drawdown and drawdown duration | Survivability and psychological tolerance |
| Calmar ratio | Return / max drawdown |
| Trade count and turnover | Statistical power and cost drag |
| Information coefficient (IC) | Rank correlation between signal and outcome, useful for comparing models |
| Lift over base rate | Hit rate vs. the unconditional rate. Essential when the target event is rare (top gainers are rare). |
| Net-of-cost vs. gross | Whether the edge survives realistic costs |
| Stability across time/regimes | Performance by year, volatility regime, and market trend |

Always report these **net of costs** and with **confidence intervals**, and check whether results hold across subperiods.

## 11. Honest caveats

- **"Proven" means** replicated out-of-sample, after costs, across time periods, and not decayed after publication. Few short-term gainer-prediction strategies meet that bar. Many documented anomalies have shrunk substantially.
- **Gainer-chasing is often a losing strategy.** Stocks that spike on high volume frequently reverse, and lottery-like stocks tend to underperform on average. Your study may well find that fading spikes beats chasing them, but test it.
- **Base rates dominate.** If top gainers are 1% of observations, a model that's "right 60% of the time" may just be predicting "not a gainer" poorly. Always compare to the base rate and report precision and recall.
- **Regime dependence.** Crypto, in particular, can behave completely differently in bull markets, bear markets, and low-liquidity periods.
- **If you plan to sell signals,** be aware that providing trading recommendations for compensation can trigger regulatory requirements (e.g., investment adviser registration in the US), and selling performance claims without rigorous verification creates legal and reputational risk. Hypothetical or backtested results must be clearly disclosed as such.

## Practical starting plan

1. Build a survivorship-free dataset for one asset class (US large-cap equities is a good first choice because data is cleanest).
2. Run an event study on past top gainers vs. matched controls at 1-day and 1-week horizons.
3. Define a triple-barrier label with a few TP/SL multiples and a time limit.
4. Train a simple pooled model with walk-forward validation, net of realistic costs.
5. Apply the deflated Sharpe and permutation tests to see whether results survive.
6. If they do, paper trade for at least 3 months with frozen signals.
7. Only then repeat for crypto and forex, using the same pipeline so the comparison is fair.

The most valuable outcome of this process is often knowing reliably that a candidate edge is *not* real. Build the pipeline so that's easy to detect.

### 🔍 1. Which Asset Class is More Predictable?
**Short answer:** None are "predictable" in a deterministic sense. Predictability is **probabilistic, regime-dependent, and cost-adjusted**. Here’s how they compare:

| Asset Class | Structural Edge | Main Challenge | Direction Bias |
|-------------|----------------|----------------|----------------|
| **Forex (Majors)** | Highly efficient, tight spreads, strong mean-reversion in ranges & clean trends in regimes | Low net-of-cost alpha, algorithmic dominance, macro-driven noise | Long/short roughly symmetric; harder to exploit structural drift |
| **Large-Cap Equities** | Equity risk premium creates long-term upward drift; earnings/sector rotation create repeatable catalysts | Survivorship bias, corporate action complexity, institutional crowding | **Longs easier** than shorts due to structural drift & buy-side pressure |
| **Small/Mid-Cap & Thematic Equities** | Less efficiently priced, higher retail/institutional flow imbalance, earnings elasticity | Liquidity slippage, short borrow costs, event gaps | Direction depends on catalyst; momentum works well but reverses fast post-spike |
| **Crypto** | Lower microstructural efficiency, asymmetric cycles (halvings, network upgrades, leverage flushes) | High volatility, exchange risk, funding costs, narrative-driven runs | **Trend-following longs historically stronger**; shorts require volatility regime filtering |
| **Mutual Funds / ETFs** | Passive indexing, NAV lag, high fees, daily pricing | Not suited for daily/weekly signals; NAV doesn't reflect intraday moves | ❌ Generally excluded from short-term signal engines |

**Verdict:**  
- **Highest alpha potential (pre-cost):** Crypto & thematic equities  
- **Most stable/robust (post-cost):** Major forex & large-cap indices  
- **Direction asymmetry:** Long bias dominates in equity/crypto; forex is near-symmetric but heavily regime-dependent.

---
### 📐 2. Is a Specific "Symbol" More Predictable, or Symbol + Direction?
- **Symbol-specific predictability exists** but is **contextual**:
  - BTC/ETH show cyclicality around halvings, on-chain liquidity shifts, and funding rate extremes.
  - Mega-cap tech (NVDA, MSFT) react predictably to earnings, guidance, and sector rotation.
  - Forex pairs (EURUSD, GBPUSD) show cleaner technical responses due to standardized liquidity pools.
- **Symbol + Direction** is the real predictive unit. A ticker's "top gainer" status is only actionable if you know:
  - The underlying driver (catalyst, volume surge, relative strength vs sector/index)
  - The likely continuation vs reversal regime
  - Volatility environment (expanding vs contracting)
- **Rule of thumb:** Predictability isn't intrinsic to the symbol. It's an emergent property of **symbol × timeframe × regime × microstructure**.

---
### 🧠 3. Signal Generation & TP/SL Framework
Avoid fixed % TP/SL. Use dynamic, volatility-aware exits:

| Component | Recommended Approach |
|----------|----------------------|
| **Entry Filter** | Relative strength (vs benchmark/sector), volume confirmation (>1.5x 20-day avg), volatility expansion break, regime classifier (HMM or rolling correlation/vol regime) |
| **TP Strategy** | ATR×2–3, swing high/low, Fibonacci extensions, or dynamic trail (chandelier exit). Use partial TP + runner for trends. |
| **SL Strategy** | ATR×1–1.5 below invalidation level, structure break (lower low for longs), or volatility contraction stop. Never place SL in known liquidity sweeps zones. |
| **Position Sizing** | Fractional Kelly or volatility targeting (risk per trade ≤ 1–2% of equity). Adjust for correlation & drawdown control. |
| **Exit Logic** | Time-based decay (e.g., close after 5 days if target not hit), momentum exhaustion (RSI divergence, volume drop), or regime shift signal. |

**Critical:** Signals must survive **net-of-cost backtesting**. If gross expectancy is < 0.8, it will fail live.

---
### 📉 4. Backtesting: How to Do It Right
Retail backtests are almost always wrong. Institutional ones follow these rules:

1. **Data Quality:**
   - Use **Point-in-Time (PIT)** databases to avoid survivorship bias.
   - Adjust for splits, dividends, delistings, exchange halts.
   - Include microtransaction data: bid-ask spread, slippage model, commissions, funding rates (crypto), borrow costs (shorts).

2. **Validation Architecture:**
   - **Walk-Forward Optimization** (e.g., train on 6mo, validate on 2mo, roll forward)
   - **Purged k-fold cross-validation** (block time series, remove leakage around folds)
   - **Regime-split testing**: Bull, bear, sideways, high-vol, low-vol periods
   - **Monte Carlo permutation** on trade order to assess path dependency risk

3. **Avoid These Traps:**
   - Look-ahead bias (using future news/volume/NAV)
   - Over-optimization (use regularization, parameter stability checks)
   - Ignoring capacity (liquidity limits per symbol/timeframe)

---
### 🔄 5. Forward Testing: From Paper to Live
Backtest ≠ production. Forward testing bridges the gap:

| Phase | Duration | Focus |
|-------|----------|-------|
| **Paper Trading** | 2–4 weeks | Exact replica of backtest logic, real-time data, simulated fills, latency/spread injection |
| **Live Simulation** | 1–3 months | Broker/API sandbox, real execution logs, track slippage vs model, monitor fill quality |
| **Small Live Capital** | 3–6 months | 1–5% of intended size. Track decay, regime shifts, model drift. Scale only if forward Sharpe > 0.5 & max DD < backtest DD × 1.2 |

**Forward Test Metrics to Track:**
- Fill rate & slippage vs expected
- Latency & order routing impact
- Model performance decay (rolling 30d Sharpe/expectancy)
- Correlation breakdown (why did signal fail?)
- Capacity utilization per symbol

### 📊 6. Key Performance Metrics (What Actually Matters)
Ignore raw returns. Measure this stack:

| Category | Metrics | Target Benchmarks |
|----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Return** | CAGR, Annualized Return | Consistent > risk-free rate |
| **Risk-Adjusted** | Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar, Omega | Sharpe ≥ 1.0, Sortino ≥ 1.5, Calmar ≥ 0.8 |
| **Trade-Level** | Win Rate, Profit Factor, Expectancy, Avg R-multiple | PF ≥ 1.3, Expectancy > 0, Avg R ≥ 1.0 |
| **Risk/Dynamic** | Max Drawdown, Max Consecutive Losses, Recovery Time | DD < 20%, Recovery < 6mo |
| **Operational** | Turnover, Hit Rate vs Edge, Slippage Impact, Capacity Constraints | Turnover < 30%/day, DD increase < 15% post-cost |
| **Statistical** | p-value on returns, Bootstrap CI, t-stat on expectancy | p < 0.05, CI excludes zero, t > 2 |

**Gold Standard:** If your strategy survives out-of-sample walk-forward, forward paper/live testing, and shows positive expectancy after full costs, low turnover, and controlled drawdowns, **it has an edge**. Call it "proven".

### ⚠️ Critical Reality Checks
1. **"Top Gainer" is a mean-reversion trap.** Daily winners often fade 2–5 days later. You must filter for **continuation regime** (volume persistence, sector leadership, volatility expansion, catalyst freshness).
2. **Edges decay.** What works today will be arbitraged or structurally altered in 6–18 months. Continuous feature drift monitoring is mandatory.

4. **Direction matters more than timing.** In trending regimes, patience > precision. In ranging regimes, mean-reversion > breakout.
5. **No strategy is "proven" forever.** Professional systems treat edges as perishable assets. They're deployed, monitored, decayed, and replaced.

## GLM 5.3 Flash

Below is the playbook I'd actually follow, including how I'd answer each of your sub-questions with defensible evidence rather than vibes.

## 0. Reframe the problem first

"Predict tomorrow's top gainers" is a near-impossible framing — it's a selection effect (you're seeing the extreme right tail *after* it happened). The tractable version is:

> **"Rank the tradable universe by probability of a large move over the next 1–5 days, in a given direction, with an acceptable expectancy after costs."**

That's a cross-sectional ranking / classification problem with event-detection features — and it's how everything below is structured.

## 1. Reverse-engineer the drivers (hypothesis library)

Top gainers are almost always some combination of, days before or hours before the move:

- **Flow/momentum:** 1–5 day momentum, breakout from consolidation, proximity to 52-week high, relative strength vs. sector and market
- **Volume anomalies:** unusual volume vs. 20-day average, volume-on-up-days ratio, gaps
- **Catalysts:** earnings dates/surprises, guidance, analyst revisions, insider buys, ETF inclusion, index rebalances
- **Structural/squeeze:** low float, high short interest, days-to-cover (stocks); funding rates, open interest, liquidation cascades (crypto)
- **Sentiment/news NLP:** headline sentiment velocity, social volume spikes (StockTwits/X/Reddit), Google Trends breaks
- **Crypto-specific:** funding rate flips, OI buildup, stablecoin inflows, exchange outflows, BTC dominance shifts, on-chain MVRV
- **Macro/market regime:** breadth, VIX regime, risk-on/off — because the *same signal* behaves differently in trend vs. chop

You don't need all of these. You need to test which ones have **out-of-sample information** for *your* horizon and universe.

## 2. Which asset class is "more predictable"?

**Method (not opinion):** run the identical pipeline — same features, labels, costs, horizon — per asset class and compare out-of-sample statistics (IC, Sharpe after costs). Then rank.

**Priors from the literature/practice** (you verify, don't assume):

| Asset class | Expected predictability | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Small/mid-cap stocks, event-driven | Highest | Momentum, earnings drift, squeezes, but capacity is small and slippage is brutal |
| Crypto | High *volatility*, decent signal | Momentum + funding/flow anomalies exist; signal-to-noise is often better than equities intraday; 24/7 data helps |
| Large-cap equities | Moderate | Efficient, momentum works only cross-sectionally over weeks |
| Forex | Hardest at short horizons | Near random-walk, spreads eat most edges; carry/trend work only on longer horizons |
| Mutual funds | Not really tradeable | T+1 NAV, once-daily pricing — their "top gainers" are just factor/sector bets you can't front-run; skip |

Key subtlety: crypto's higher volatility doesn't automatically mean more predictable — what matters is **signal-to-noise ratio and post-cost expectancy**, which is exactly why you test with identical cost assumptions.

## 3. Symbol-level vs. symbol + direction predictability

Do both, formally:

- **Per-symbol OOS stats:** rolling IC (correlation of signal vs. forward return), hit rate, profit factor per symbol. Rank symbols by **ICIR** (IC mean / IC std), not average IC — consistency matters.
- **Direction asymmetry:** run long-only and short-only variants separately. Expect real asymmetries:
  - Shorts have borrow costs, buy-in risk, and suffer in squeezes
  - Equities fall faster than they rise (crash asymmetry) → short edges exist but fat-tailed
  - Crypto: long vs. short tilts with funding rates and open interest positioning
- **Regime conditioning:** a symbol may be predictable only in trend regimes (ADX filter) or high-vol regimes. Split OOS performance by regime before concluding anything.

One caution: if you test 500 symbols × 2 directions × 10 signals, you'll find "significant" symbols by chance. Use multiple-testing corrections (deflated Sharpe ratio, White's Reality Check / SPA test) — otherwise you're discovering noise.

## 4. Labels: build TP/SL *into* the labeling (triple-barrier)

Since your output is a TP/SL signal, don't label outcomes as "up or down" — label with the **triple-barrier method** (López de Prado):

For each entry, set three barriers and see which is touched first:
- **TP barrier:** e.g., +2 × ATR(14)
- **SL barrier:** e.g., −1.5 × ATR(14)
- **Time barrier:** e.g., 5 trading days (exit at market)

Label = which barrier hit first (+1 / −1 / 0). This makes your model optimize *exactly* the trade you'll actually take. Bonus: run **MAE/MFE analysis** — for winners, what's the max adverse excursion before TP? That tells you empirically where the SL belongs (just beyond the MAE distribution of winners); TP belongs near the upper quantile of the MFE distribution. You'll find 1.5–3× ATR stops and 2–4× ATR targets are common starting points, but let the data set them per symbol/horizon.

## 5. Back-testing properly (where most people fool themselves)

Non-negotiables:

1. **Point-in-time data:** survivorship-free universe (include delisted symbols!), split/dividend-adjusted prices, fundamentals and news timestamped at *release*, not period end. Look-ahead bias is the #1 killer.
2. **Realistic costs:** spread, commission, slippage (worse for small caps — model impact ∝ √(notional/ADV)), borrow fees for shorts, funding rates for crypto perps. Re-test the whole strategy at 2× your cost estimate; if it dies, it was never real.
3. **No overlapping leakage:** triple-barrier labels overlap in time → use **purged K-fold with embargo** or **combinatorial purged CV (CPCV)**, or simple walk-forward (train on years 1–5, test on year 6; roll forward).
4. **Control the number of trials.** Log every experiment. With enough backtests you'll always find a great-looking one by luck. Use the **Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO)** and **Deflated Sharpe Ratio** (adjusts for how many variants you tried).
5. **Event-driven backtest** over vectorized if your logic is path-dependent (stops get touched intraday — you need intraday highs/lows, not just closes).

**What counts as "proven":**
- Positive OOS expectancy after full costs across ≥2 disjoint out-of-sample periods
- t-stat of per-trade returns > 2 (ideally > 3), bootstrap CI excluding zero
- Deflated Sharpe > 0 after accounting for trials tried
- OOS Sharpe ≥ ~50–60% of IS Sharpe (bigger decay = overfit)
- PBO < ~30%
- And even then: "proven" = "not yet disproven." Edges decay; monitoring is part of the system.

## 6. Forward-testing

Staged, with a comparison loop:

1. **Paper trade / sim** for 1–3 months on live data. Log every fill vs. the simulated fill — this measures your real slippage model.
2. **Shadow portfolio:** if you have limited capital, run the full paper portfolio alongside a small live one.
3. **Small live capital** with size at 25% of intended risk, scaling up only if live tracks expectation.
4. **Compare distributions, not just P&L:** hit rate, average win/loss, slippage live vs. modeled, signal decay (IC of live signals vs. backtest IC).
5. **Drift monitoring:** Population Stability Index / KS-test on feature distributions; alert when the live regime leaves your training regime support.

## 7. The metrics (organized)

**Predictive quality:**
- IC (Spearman, signal vs. forward return) and ICIR
- Hit rate vs. base rate; Brier score / log-loss (calibration of probabilities)
- PR-AUC (better than ROC-AUC for rare "big mover" events)
- Precision@K (of your top-K picks, how many actually became big movers — the honest "top gainers" metric)

**Trade-level (drives TP/SL design):**
- Expectancy: E = p·avgWin − (1−p)·avgLoss
- Profit factor (gross wins / gross losses), payoff ratio
- TP-hit / SL-hit / timeout distribution
- MAE and MFE distributions (stop placement)
- Avg time-in-trade

**Portfolio/system:**
- CAGR, annualized vol, Sharpe — report **Deflated Sharpe**
- Sortino, Calmar, max drawdown, time-under-water
- Tail: CVaR(5%), worst trade
- Turnover, capacity (max notional before impact destroys the edge)
- OOS/IS decay ratio

**Execution (live only):**
- Slippage vs. model (per symbol and per volatility regime)
- Fill rate, partial fills, rejected orders

## 8. Concrete workflow, end to end

1. Define universe + tradability rules (price > $2, ADV > $1M, borrowable if shorting; top ~200 liquid crypto pairs).
2. Build PIT data infra; corporate actions, timestamps.
3. Generate triple-barrier labels per asset class.
4. Build the feature/hypothesis library from §1.
5. Model: start dumb — a composite z-score ranking. Then gradient boosting or logistic regression with purged CV. The simple model is your benchmark; anything complex must beat it OOS.
6. Backtest event-driven with full costs → iterate with a strict experiment budget.
7. Walk-forward + CPCV; compute deflated Sharpe, PBO.
8. Rank asset classes and (symbol × direction) cells by OOS ICIR and post-cost expectancy. That ranking *is* your answer to "which is more predictable."
9. Forward test: paper → small live → scale, with drift monitoring and a hard kill-switch (e.g., 1.5× historical max drawdown → halt and re-research).

## Honest caveats

- The slippage on chasing movers is worst exactly when the signal is strongest — pumps are where retail gets filled worst. Your backtest must punish you for this.
- Regimes change; a "proven" edge is a perishable asset. Build the monitoring before you build the strategy.
- Most of the realistic retail-accessible edge lives in small-cap/event-driven equities and crypto momentum/funding. Forex short-term and mutual funds are mostly not worth the effort for this purpose.

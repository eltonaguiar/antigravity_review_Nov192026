Reverse‑Engineering "Top Gainers": A Research Protocol

This is a well‑posed question, but it contains a trap that kills ~90% of attempts before any modeling happens. Let me start there, then build the full protocol.

1. The Core Trap: Gainers Lists Are Conditioned on the Outcome

A "top gainers" list is a sample drawn on the dependent variable. If you collect 5,000 historical gainers and find that 78% had a volume spike, a tight consolidation, and a low float — you have learned P(features | big move), not P(big move | features). Those are different by a factor of the base rate, which is usually brutal.

If 1,500 of 3,000 names had a volume spike but only 200 became gainers, your "78% pattern" has a ~13% hit rate against a ~2% base rate. That's a real 6× lift — but it is not a 78% system, and the difference determines whether your TP/SL geometry is survivable.

So do it as a proper case‑control study first, then a cohort study:

Stage	Design	Output
Reverse‑engineering	Cases = historical top‑N gainers. Controls = matched non‑gainers (match on date, market cap bucket, sector, price level, trailing vol, liquidity).	Conditional logistic regression → odds ratios per antecedent feature. Tells you what discriminates.
Cohort / prospective	Full point‑in‑time universe, every day, including losers and flat names. Label everything.	Base rates, precision@K, lift, calibration. Tells you how often you're right.
Trading	Add costs, path, sizing, correlation.	Tells you whether it's worth doing.

Matching controls is the step everyone skips. Without it, you'll "discover" that gainers are volatile small caps — which is a description of the universe, not an edge.

2. Define the Target Precisely (This Is Where TP/SL Enters)

"Predict top gainers" is ambiguous. Pick one — they need different models and different metrics:

Cross‑sectional ranking — rank the universe by forward return over horizon h; you care about the top decile or top‑K. Metric: IC, precision@K, NDCG@K.
Event classification — P(return > threshold within h). Threshold should be volatility‑normalized (e.g., > 2.5σ), not a fixed 10%, or you'll just be predicting "which assets are volatile."
Path‑dependent / triple‑barrier — does price hit +TP before −SL before time limit T? This is the label that matches your actual trade, and it's what you want if you're issuing TP/SL signals.
Meta‑labeling — a primary model picks direction; a secondary model predicts P(that primary signal is profitable). The secondary model is a binary classifier and is far easier to fit and calibrate. This is usually the highest‑ROI architecture for signal generation.

Critical: collect MFE/MAE for every candidate entry, not just the terminal return:

MFE (Maximum Favorable Excursion) = max unrealized profit over the holding window
MAE (Maximum Adverse Excursion) = max unrealized drawdown over the window

The joint distribution of (MFE, MAE, time‑to‑MFE, time‑to‑MAE), conditioned on your signal, is your TP/SL calibration data. Everything about barrier placement falls out of it. Terminal returns alone throw away the path, and the path is what stops you out.

2def triple_barrier(px, entry_t, tp_mult, sl_mult, sigma, max_bars):

3    up   = entry_px * (1 + tp_mult * sigma)

4    dn   = entry_px * (1 - sl_mult * sigma)

5    for t in range(entry_t+1, entry_t+max_bars+1):

6        # if BOTH barriers inside one bar -> ambiguous, resolve with sub-bars

7        hit_up, hit_dn = bar.high >= up, bar.low <= dn

8        if hit_up and hit_dn: return resolve_with_lower_timeframe(...)

9        if hit_dn: return -1, t-entry_t   # check SL first (conservative)

10        if hit_up: return +1, t-entry_t

11    return sign(px[T]-entry_px), max_bars
3. The Uncomfortable Theorem About TP/SL

For a driftless martingale, no combination of TP, SL, trailing stop, or breakeven move produces positive expectancy. Optional stopping theorem: with bounded barriers and a time limit, E[P&L at stop] = 0, minus costs. Barriers reshape the distribution — they trade win rate against payoff ratio — they do not create edge.

This means:

A 90% win rate with TP=0.5 ATR / SL=5 ATR is not a strategy; it's a short‑volatility position with a hidden tail.
Every "TP/SL optimizer" that shows a profitable grid on random‑walk data is overfitting or leaking.
Your edge must come from one of four places: (a) conditional drift — the signal genuinely shifts E[r]; (b) non‑martingale path structure — autocorrelation, vol clustering, mean reversion; (c) a risk premium you're being paid to bear; (d) genuine convexity/optionality.

Practical test: run your entire TP/SL optimization pipeline on (i) bootstrapped/shuffled returns preserving vol clustering, and (ii) synthetic GBM matched to each symbol's σ. Whatever "edge" appears there is your overfitting floor. Subtract it. This single control experiment is worth more than most indicator libraries.

Also: risk premia are not free. If your long signal earns the equity risk premium, you must benchmark against buy‑and‑hold, not against zero.

4. Data & Universe Hygiene (Differs Wildly by Asset Class)
Asset class	Killers you must handle
US equities	Survivorship (delisted names, including delisting returns), point‑in‑time fundamentals (no backfilled restatements), retroactively adjusted price histories (split/dividend adjustments silently change "distance from 52w high" as computed years ago), LULD halts, borrow availability + fee for shorts, SSR/uptick rules, corporate actions, index reconstitution dates.
Crypto	Exchange survivorship (dead coins/tokens are the majority), wash‑traded volume, delistings, perps vs spot basis, funding accrual, exchange outages during exactly the moves you're modeling, stablecoin depegs, token unlocks, chain‑level timestamp vs exchange timestamp.
FX	No consolidated tape — your broker's feed ≠ another's; volume is tick count, not real volume; session/rollover artifacts; weekend gaps; swap/financing; peg breaks and intervention tails; asymmetric liquidity by hour.
Mutual funds	Essentially untradable for this purpose. Forward pricing (you get the next NAV, not the one you see), stale pricing of illiquid holdings creates artificial autocorrelation that looks like predictability but isn't harvestable, redemption fees/lockups, no intraday, no shorting. Substitute the corresponding ETF or the underlying holdings. Any "predictable mutual fund NAV" result is almost certainly a stale‑price artifact.

Two universal rules: (1) every feature must be computable from information available at the decision timestamp, with a logged availability lag; (2) store an append‑only, versioned snapshot so a backtest run in 2027 reproduces the 2025 data exactly.

5. Is Predictability a Property of the Asset, the Symbol, or the Symbol×Direction?

None of them alone. Predictability is a property of the tuple:

(symbol, horizon, feature set, label, cost structure, regime)

Change the horizon from 1 day to 20 days and momentum flips to reversal. Change costs from 1bp to 30bps and a real edge becomes unprofitable. So measure it as a surface, not a scalar.

A hierarchy of measurements, cheapest first

Tier 1 — Model‑free structure (no fitting, no overfitting risk):

Statistic	What it tells you
Variance ratio VR(q) = Var(r_q)/(q·Var(r_1))	>1 trending, <1 mean‑reverting. Lo–MacKinlay gives a proper test stat with heteroskedasticity‑robust SEs.
Hurst exponent (DFA, not naive R/S)	Persistence vs anti‑persistence. Beware: estimator bias is large on short samples.
Return autocorrelation / Ljung–Box at lags 1..k	Direct linear predictability.
Volatility predictability (GARCH R², HAR‑RV R²)	Almost always much higher than return predictability. This is the honest answer to "what's predictable": vol is far more forecastable than direction.
Cross‑sectional dispersion	Size of the prize. High dispersion = more separation between winners and losers = easier ranking problem.
Approximate entropy / compression ratio	Crude, but a fast screen for structure.
Mutual information between candidate features and forward labels	Catches nonlinear dependence that correlation misses.

Tier 2 — Simple model, honest OOS: fit a small regularized model (ridge / shallow GBM, ≤10 features) per symbol and per horizon with purged CV. Record out‑of‑sample IC, AUC/PR‑AUC, and Brier score. Compare against a per‑symbol naive baseline (historical base rate, or drift‑only). Skill = performance above the naive baseline, not above 50%.

Tier 3 — Economic: does the statistical edge survive costs, and what's the capacity?

Comparing asset classes fairly

Do not compare raw accuracy. A 55% hit rate on a 2:1 payoff and a 70% hit rate on a 0.3:1 payoff are not comparable. Normalize everything to:

Cost‑adjusted expectancy per unit of risk (R)
Deflated Sharpe (accounting for how many variants you tried per class)
Capacity ($ deployable before impact eats the alpha)
IC per unit of turnover — a small edge traded 500×/year may beat a big edge traded 5×/year, or may die on costs
Honest priors (to be tested, not assumed)
Class	Raw dispersion / apparent predictability	Realizability	Net assessment for a "top gainers" hunt
Micro/small‑cap equities	Highest — this is where 40% days live	Worst: halts, no borrow, 100–300bp round trips, pump contamination, extreme survivorship	Highest apparent edge, lowest realizable. Great for research, brutal live.
Crypto alts (mid‑cap, liquid perps)	Very high; retail‑driven flow; free funding/OI/liquidation data that is genuinely informative	Medium: 24/7 (no gap risk), easy shorting via perps, but fees + counterparty risk	Probably the best hunting ground at small size. Data hygiene is the hard part.
Large‑cap equities	Low‑moderate; heavily arbitraged	Excellent: data, borrow, costs, rich anomaly literature	Best place to validate methodology; hardest place to find new alpha.
FX majors	Lowest dispersion; near‑efficient at short horizons	Best costs/liquidity, but needs leverage; carry & trend work at longer horizons	Predictability is horizon‑dependent: poor intraday, decent multi‑week (carry + TSMOM).
Mutual funds	Artificially high (stale NAV)	~Zero for short‑term trading	Exclude. Use ETFs.

The designed experiment: run one identical pipeline across all classes, with per‑class cost models and per‑class universes, and compare cost‑adjusted, deflated, capacity‑adjusted OOS metrics with bootstrap confidence intervals. Anything else is comparing your data quality, not the market.

6. Symbol vs. Symbol × Direction — Asymmetry Is Real and Structural

Yes, direction matters, and not just statistically — there are mechanisms:

Equities

Leverage effect: volatility rises as price falls → downside moves are faster and larger in σ‑units. Same ATR multiple ≠ same risk on both sides.
Positive unconditional drift → long base rate > short base rate. Your classifier must be compared to different baselines per side.
Short‑term reversal is stronger after down moves; PEAD is stronger for positive surprises in some regimes.
Shorting constraints (borrow fee, availability, SSR) make the short side's realizable edge much lower than its statistical edge.

Crypto

Liquidation cascades are downside‑asymmetric: forced selling begets forced selling. Down moves cluster and overshoot; up moves are more often gradual (except listing/announcement pumps).
Funding skew: crowded longs vs crowded shorts have different squeeze dynamics. Perp funding is itself a directional feature.

FX

"Up the stairs, down the elevator" in risk/carry currencies — unwind is faster than accumulation.
Central bank intervention and peg breaks are structurally one‑sided.
How to test it properly

Don't reflexively split your data — you halve your sample and double your variance.

Fit one pooled model with a side indicator and side × feature interactions.
Test whether the interaction terms add OOS value (nested CV, likelihood ratio, or simply ΔIC on held‑out folds).
Check calibration separately per side. This is the most common real finding: the model is well‑calibrated for longs and systematically overconfident for shorts.
Report IC_long, IC_short, precision@K_long, precision@K_short, and Brier per side.
Only split into separate models if there's evidence of parameter instability and enough samples per side (rule of thumb: ≥ a few hundred independent events per side, more if labels overlap).
Even with a pooled model, fit TP/SL separately per side — the MFE/MAE distributions genuinely differ, and this is cheap to do without overfitting because you're estimating distributions, not searching a parameter grid.

Practical expectation: short signals typically want tighter time limits and smaller TP in ATR units (moves complete faster), while long signals tolerate longer holds. Encode that as a prior rather than letting a grid search discover it from noise.

7. Setting TP/SL Empirically (Without Overfitting)

Step 1 — Volatility‑normalize everything. TP and SL in units of ATR or forecast σ (from GARCH/HAR), never fixed percentages. A 5% stop is a different object on BTC vs EURUSD vs a $2 microcap.

Step 2 — Estimate the conditional MFE/MAE distributions given your signal, per side, per regime. From these you can compute, for any (tp, sl, T) triple, the empirical hit probabilities and the expectancy — without re‑running a full backtest per grid point.

Step 3 — Map the expectancy surface, but treat it as a multiple‑testing problem:

Fit a smooth surface (GP or low‑order polynomial) over the grid rather than picking the argmax cell.
Require a plateau, not a peak. If TP=2.1 ATR works and 2.0/2.2 don't, it's noise.
Report the distribution of performance across the neighborhood, not the best point.

Step 4 — Anchor to a null. For GBM with drift μ and vol σ, first‑passage probabilities are analytic (reflection principle / inverse Gaussian). Compute the martingale‑null expectancy for each barrier pair. Your empirical edge is the difference from that null, not the raw number.

Step 5 — Sanity checks:

Expectancy: E = p·W − (1−p)·L − costs. Breakeven win rate for reward:risk R is 1/(1+R) before costs. Print this table; it disciplines everything.
Stops slip. Model stop fills at stop_price − k·σ_spread in fast markets, worse on gaps. Assume gap‑through fills at the open, not the stop.
Ambiguous bars: if TP and SL both sit inside one bar's range, you must resolve with lower‑timeframe data or assume SL first. Assuming TP first is the single most common inflator of TP/SL backtests.
Prefer fewer parameters: fixed TP/SL + time stop beats a trailing/breakeven/scale‑out contraption with 7 knobs unless you can prove each knob's marginal OOS contribution.

Step 6 — Sizing. Fractional Kelly (¼ to ½ of full), volatility targeting, correlation‑aware exposure caps. Full Kelly on estimated parameters is effectively a bet that your estimates are exact. They aren't.

8. What Counts as "Proven"?

An evidence ladder. Most published/retail systems sit at rung 1–2.

Rung	Evidence	Value
0	Screenshots, testimonials, equity curves without trades	Zero
1	In‑sample backtest	~Zero
2	Single holdout OOS	Weak — one draw from a noisy distribution
3	Walk‑forward + purged/embargoed CPCV, distribution of Sharpes, PBO	Meaningful
4	Multiple‑testing corrected: Deflated Sharpe, Hansen SPA / White Reality Check across all trials	Strong statistical
5	Pre‑registered paper trading on live data, timestamped predictions	Strong — catches leakage, staleness, latency
6	Live small capital, fills reconciled vs. modeled	Strongest — catches impact, borrow, funding, halts
7	Scaled, stable across ≥2 regimes, capacity curve mapped	Deployable

Plus a mechanism prior. Before you fit anything, write down why the edge should exist: a risk premium, a behavioral bias, a structural/institutional constraint (mandates, rebalancing, margin), or slow information diffusion. A signal with no mechanism has a low prior probability of being real, and Bayesian reasoning says you should demand far more evidence for it.

Time‑series momentum (12m), cross‑sectional momentum (with crash risk), short‑term reversal, post‑earnings‑announcement drift, low‑vol/BAB, FX carry, value, index rebalance effects, and crypto basis/funding carry. 9. Backtesting Protocol

Cross‑validation

Labels overlap in time → standard K‑fold leaks. Use purged K‑fold with embargo (drop training samples whose label window overlaps the test window, plus a buffer).
Better: Combinatorial Purged CV (CPCV) — generates many train/test paths, yielding a distribution of Sharpe and a Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO). If PBO > ~0.5, your selection process is picking noise.
Walk‑forward (rolling and anchored) as a separate, realistic simulation of retraining cadence.

Costs — model them, don't hand‑wave

Spread + commission + fees
Slippage as a function of participation rate and volatility; impact ≈ Y·σ·√(Q/ADV) (square‑root law)
Short borrow fees and recall risk
Perp funding accrual (can dominate a multi‑day crypto signal)
Financing/margin
Then stress at 2× and 3×. If the edge dies at 1.5× costs, it isn't real.

Robustness battery

Parameter perturbation (±20% on every knob) → performance should degrade smoothly
Execution delay (1 bar, 5 min, next open) → a signal that only works at the exact close is suspect
Remove the top 5% of trades and the best 10 days → does it survive?
Stationary/block bootstrap for CIs (returns aren't IID)
Newey–West HAC t‑stats for overlapping returns
Regime slices: vol terciles, trend/range, bull/bear, pre/post‑2020, rate regimes, liquidity regimes
Synthetic‑data control (Section 3)

Minimum sample. Use Minimum Track Record Length logic: with a modest Sharpe and fat tails, you may need years of live data to reject zero skill at conventional confidence. Compute this number before you start forward testing so you know what you're signing up for.

10. Forward Testing — Treat It Like a Clinical Trial

Pre‑register. Write and timestamp a document containing: hypothesis and mechanism, universe, horizon, entry rule, TP/SL rule, sizing, primary metric, minimum detectable effect, required sample size, stopping rule, and kill criteria. Then freeze the model artifact and hash it.

Commit to predictions. Every signal is written to an append‑only log before the outcome is known, with: timestamp, symbol, side, entry ref, TP, SL, time limit, predicted probability, model hash, feature snapshot hash. Periodically publish the hash of the log. This makes retrospective fitting impossible — including by you, to yourself, which is the more common failure.

Three phases:

Shadow (4–8 weeks): live data, real timestamps, no orders. Reconcile every predicted fill vs. what the market actually did. This is where you discover your feature pipeline used a value that wasn't available yet.
Micro‑live: smallest viable size. Now you measure real slippage, borrow, funding, rejects, halts, and latency. Compare live fills to modeled fills — the gap is your backtest's bias, quantified.
Ramp: increase size along a measured capacity curve; stop when marginal impact eats a set fraction of gross alpha.

Sequential testing, not peeking. Repeatedly checking "is it significant yet?" inflates false positives. Use alpha‑spending (O'Brien–Fleming), SPRT, or always‑valid e‑values/confidence sequences. Pre‑commit to the stopping rule.

Drift monitoring in production:

Feature distribution shift: PSI, KS tests vs. training distribution
Rolling IC and rolling calibration (reliability curve)
Realized vs. predicted slippage
CUSUM / Page–Hinkley change detection on the P&L residual → automatic de‑risking
Hard kill switches: max daily loss, max DD, max exposure, data‑staleness timeout

Reconciliation is the whole point: if live and shadow diverge, the difference is a bug or an unmodeled cost — not "the market changed." Investigate before scaling.

11. The Metrics Dashboard
A. Prediction quality (is there information?)
Metric	Why
Spearman IC (per period), ICIR = mean(IC)/std(IC)	The workhorse for cross‑sectional ranking
Precision@K and lift = precision@K ÷ (K/N)	The metric for "top gainers." You only need the top K to be right.
PR‑AUC (preferred over ROC‑AUC under heavy imbalance)	Rare‑event discrimination
Brier score / log loss, calibration curve, ECE	Essential — you can't size positions from uncalibrated probabilities
Hit rate vs. base rate, per side	Skill ≠ accuracy
NDCG@K, decile monotonicity	Ranking quality beyond the top bucket
Conditional forward‑return distribution of picks vs. universe	Does the magnitude differ, not just the frequency?
B. Trade level (does the geometry work?)

Expectancy in R; profit factor; payoff ratio; win rate; R‑multiple distribution (fat left tail?); MAE/MFE distributions; time‑in‑trade; % of trades stopped vs. timed out vs. TP; gap‑through frequency; stop slippage.

C. Portfolio (is it worth capital?)

CAGR, vol, Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar/MAR, max DD and DD duration, Ulcer index, skew/kurtosis, tail ratio, Omega, turnover, gross/net exposure, beta and alpha vs. a relevant benchmark (long signals vs. the index, not vs. cash), correlation to existing strategies.

D. Statistical confidence (is it luck?)

Deflated Sharpe, PSR, PBO, HAC t‑stat, block‑bootstrap CIs, MinTRL, trial count N, CPCV Sharpe distribution.

E. Execution & operations

Implementation shortfall (bps), slippage vs. model, fill rate, participation rate, cost as % of gross alpha, capacity ($ at which alpha halves), signal→order latency, data staleness incidents, uptime.

F. Stability

Rolling 3m/6m IC, per‑regime breakdown, parameter drift over retraining, alpha decay half‑life, performance by symbol and by side.

Report all six panels per asset class, per symbol tier, per direction. That table is the answer to "which is more predictable."

12. Failure‑Mode Checklist
☐ Sampling on the outcome (gainers‑only datasets)
☐ Survivorship — delisted equities and dead tokens
☐ Retroactively adjusted price series creating look‑ahead
☐ Non‑point‑in‑time fundamentals
☐ Overlapping labels → inflated t‑stats
☐ Ambiguous TP/SL bars resolved optimistically
☐ Stops assumed to fill at the stop price (gaps, cascades, halts)
☐ Ignoring borrow fees, funding, financing
☐ Unaccounted trial count (the silent killer)
☐ Timezone/timestamp misalignment across sources
☐ Trading at a price you can't actually get (mutual fund forward pricing; MOC assumptions)
☐ Crypto exchange outages during the modeled move
☐ Wash‑traded volume treated as real liquidity
☐ Signal decays post‑publication and you didn't budget for it
☐ Sizing based on uncalibrated probabilities
13. Phased Build Plan
Phase	Duration	Deliverable	Gate to proceed
0	2–3 wks	PIT data + universe + cost model per class	Reproducible snapshot; survivorship handled
1	2 wks	Case‑control study on historical gainers with matched controls	≥2 features with meaningful odds ratios and a mechanism story
2	2 wks	Model‑free predictability surface: VR, Hurst, vol‑R², dispersion by class/symbol/horizon/side	Identifies where structure exists at all
3	3–4 wks	Labels (triple barrier + MFE/MAE), baseline models, purged CPCV	OOS IC/precision@K lift with PBO < 0.3
4	2 wks	TP/SL surfaces from MFE/MAE; synthetic‑null control	Plateau, not peak; edge above GBM null
5	2 wks	Full cost‑aware backtest + robustness battery + DSR	Survives 2× costs, delays, trade removal
6	4–8 wks	Pre‑registered shadow trading, hashed prediction log	Live ≈ shadow; calibration holds
7	8–12 wks	Micro‑live, reconciliation, capacity curve	Slippage within model; positive expectancy in R
8	ongoing	Ramp + drift monitoring + kill switches	—

Realistic benchmarks so you know what "good" looks like: daily cross‑sectional IC of 0.02–0.05 is a genuinely usable edge. Directional hit rates of 52–56% are normal. Precision@10 of 15–25% against a 2% base rate is excellent lift. Net Sharpe of 1–2 at modest capacity is a real, deployable strategy. A backtest showing Sharpe 4, 85% win rate, and tiny drawdown is telling you there's a bug — most likely an ambiguous‑bar resolution, a look‑ahead feature, or an outcome‑conditioned sample.

Conclusion

The honest path is: stop studying winners and start studying the full cross‑section, using matched case‑control to generate hypotheses and cohort studies to establish base rates. Encode TP/SL directly into your labels via triple barriers, and calibrate them from conditional MFE/MAE distributions rather than grid‑searching for a peak — remembering that barriers alone cannot manufacture edge from a martingale. Treat predictability as a property of (symbol, horizon, features, costs, regime), measure it model‑free before you measure it with models, and split by direction only when interactions and per‑side calibration prove you should. "Proven" means purged combinatorial CV, multiple‑testing deflation across your entire trial history, a pre‑registered hashed forward test, and live fills that reconcile with your model — with a mechanism story that makes the result plausible in the first place.

On asset class priors: mutual funds are effectively untradable for this and their apparent predictability is a stale‑pricing artifact; micro‑caps have the most raw dispersion and the least realizability; FX majors are the most efficient intraday but carry and trend persist at longer horizons; liquid crypto alts probably offer the best combination of dispersion, tradable shorting, and genuinely informative free microstructure data (funding, OI, liquidations) at small size — provided your data hygiene is ruthless. But treat that as a hypothesis to be tested with one identical pipeline and cost‑adjusted, deflated, capacity‑aware metrics, not as a conclusion.

The single highest‑value thing you can build first isn't a model — it's the timestamped, hash‑committed prediction log and the synthetic‑null control. Those two artifacts make it impossible to fool yourself, and self‑deception, not lack of cleverness, is what defeats most attempts at this.

This is a research methodology, not investment advice; any strategy that clears these gates still carries real risk of loss.

Reverse engineering daily or weekly top gainers to generate trading signals (with take-profit/stop-loss targets) across asset classes like stocks, crypto, mutual funds, and forex requires a systematic, multi-layered approach. Below is a structured methodology to achieve this, including predictability analysis, backtesting, forward-testing, and performance metrics. The goal is to identify which asset classes, symbols, or directional biases (long/short) are statistically more predictable, and to validate findings rigorously.

1. Data Collection & Preprocessing
Data Sources
Stocks: Use APIs like Alpha Vantage, Yahoo Finance, or paid services (Bloomberg, Refinitiv) for historical OHLCV (Open, High, Low, Close, Volume) data. Include fundamentals (P/E, debt/equity) if needed.
Crypto: Fetch data from Binance, CoinGecko, or CoinMarketCap APIs. Focus on liquid pairs (BTC/USDT, ETH/USDT).
Forex: Use OANDA, Dukascopy, or MetaTrader’s historical tick data.
Mutual Funds: Data is scarcer; use Morningstar, Yahoo Finance, or SEC filings (delayed).
Top Gainers Lists: Scrape or fetch from financial news sites (Yahoo Finance, Bloomberg, CoinDesk) or exchanges.
Preprocessing Steps
Clean Data: Handle missing values, adjust for splits/dividends (stocks), and account for crypto forks/airdrops.
Time Alignment: Ensure all data is in UTC or a consistent timezone.
Feature Engineering:
Technical Indicators: Moving averages (SMA, EMA), RSI, MACD, Bollinger Bands, ATR, OBV.
Volatility Measures: Standard deviation, ATR, historical volatility.
Momentum: Rate of change (ROC), Stochastic Oscillator.
Volume Analysis: Volume spikes, OBV, volume-price correlation.
Fundamental (Stocks): P/E, debt ratios, earnings surprises.
Sentiment (Crypto/Forex): Social media sentiment (Twitter, Reddit), news sentiment (NLP on financial news).
Macro Indicators (Forex): Interest rates, GDP, inflation (for longer-term trades).
2. Identify Predictable Asset Classes & Symbols
Approach to Determine Predictability

Predictability can be framed as:

Directional Accuracy: Can the model predict whether the asset will rise/fall over a horizon (e.g., next 1/7/30 days)?
Magnitude Accuracy: Can the model predict the size of the move (for TP/SL sizing)?
Volatility Clustering: Are certain assets more prone to sustained trends or reversals?
Methods to Measure Predictability
Statistical Tests:
Autocorrelation: Check if past returns predict future returns (e.g., using Ljung-Box test).
Hurst Exponent: Measures mean-reverting vs. trending behavior (H ≈ 0.5: random; H > 0.5: trending; H < 0.5: mean-reverting).
Entropy Measures: Approximate entropy or sample entropy to gauge randomness.
Machine Learning Baseline:
Train a simple classifier (e.g., logistic regression, random forest) to predict next-day direction using lagged features.
Compare accuracy to a naive benchmark (e.g., "always predict up" or "predict based on recent trend").
Market Regime Analysis:
Use Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) or clustering (k-means) to identify regimes (trending vs. ranging) where predictability varies.
Example: Stocks may be more predictable in trending regimes (high H), while forex may revert in mean-reverting regimes.
Initial Findings (Empirical Evidence)
Stocks: Large-cap stocks (e.g., S&P 500 components) tend to be more predictable due to higher liquidity and institutional activity. Small-caps may have higher volatility but also higher noise.
Crypto: Highly volatile and prone to momentum bursts, but also mean-reverting in short horizons. Bitcoin and Ethereum are more predictable than altcoins due to liquidity.
Forex: Major pairs (EUR/USD, USD/JPY) are more predictable than exotic pairs due to lower slippage and higher efficiency. Carry trades (high-yielding currencies) may show longer-term trends.
Mutual Funds: Less predictable due to smoothing of returns (NAV-based pricing) and lower liquidity. Focus on sector-specific funds (e.g., tech, energy) may yield better signals.
Directional Bias: Some assets show asymmetry in predictability. For example, stocks may be easier to predict on the long side (due to uptrend bias), while forex may show better short-term reversals.
3. Signal Generation Framework
Step 1: Define the Prediction Task
Horizon: Short-term (1-7 days), medium-term (1-4 weeks), or long-term (1+ months).
Target: Binary (up/down) or continuous (return magnitude).
Direction: Long-only, short-only, or market-neutral (long/short pairs).
Step 2: Feature Selection

Use a combination of:

Technical: Trend-following (EMA crossovers, ADX), mean-reversion (RSI extremes, Bollinger Bands), volume-based (OBV spikes).
Statistical: Z-scores of returns, volatility-normalized returns (e.g., returns / ATR).
Sentiment: For crypto/forex, include sentiment scores from news/social media.
Fundamental (Stocks): Earnings surprises, revenue growth, debt ratios.
Macro (Forex): Interest rate differentials, yield curve spreads.
Step 3: Model Selection
Baseline Models:
Logistic Regression (for direction).
Random Forest / XGBoost (handles non-linearity, feature importance).
LSTM/Transformer (for sequential patterns in high-frequency data).
Ensemble Methods: Combine predictions from multiple models (e.g., technical + sentiment).
Hybrid Models: Use unsupervised learning (e.g., clustering) to segment data into regimes, then apply supervised models per regime.
Step 4: Signal Output
Direction: Long/short/neutral.
Confidence Score: Probability from the model (e.g., 65% chance of up-move).
TP/SL Levels:
TP: Based on historical average move in the predicted direction (e.g., 1.5x ATR).
SL: Based on volatility (e.g., 1x ATR) or a fixed percentage (e.g., 5%).
Trailing Stop: For trending assets, use a trailing ATR or moving average.
Position Sizing: Risk-based (e.g., 1% of capital per trade) or volatility-adjusted (e.g., inverse of ATR).
4. Backtesting Framework
Key Considerations
Data Splitting: Use walk-forward validation (e.g., 80% train, 20% test, rolling forward) to avoid lookahead bias.
Transaction Costs: Include slippage, commissions, and spreads (especially critical for crypto/forex).
Survivorship Bias: Ensure backtest data includes delisted stocks/crypto (e.g., use CRSP for stocks).
Parameter Optimization: Use techniques like Bayesian optimization or grid search, but validate on out-of-sample data.
Metrics to Evaluate
Performance Metrics:
Sharpe Ratio: Risk-adjusted return (annualized).
Sortino Ratio: Focuses on downside deviation.
Max Drawdown: Worst peak-to-trough decline.
Win Rate: % of profitable trades.
Profit Factor: Gross profits / gross losses.
Calmar Ratio: Sharpe ratio using max drawdown.
Predictability Metrics:
Accuracy: % of correct direction predictions.

1. Reframe the problem first

Studying top gainers to see what they had in common is the classic trap, because it is selection bias. Every pattern you find in winners (volume spike, breakout, news) also appears in thousands of assets that went nowhere or crashed. The real question is:

Given a set of conditions observable before the move, how does the distribution of forward returns differ from the base rate?

There are also two different problems here:

Predicting gainers before they move. This is very hard, since the moves are mostly driven by news and tail events.
Trading what happens after an asset appears on the list (continuation or reversal). This is much more tractable, because the event is observable and the sample is large.
2. The research pipeline

Data. Bad data invalidates everything downstream, so you need:

Survivorship-bias-free history, including delisted stocks and dead coins.
Point-in-time data, with no restated fundamentals or revised index membership.
Prices adjusted for splits and dividends.
Realistic cost data: spreads, fees, borrow rates, and perpetual funding rates for crypto.

Event study. For every day or week, label the top gainers (say, the top decile), then record features as of the prior close for all assets, not just the winners:

Price and volume: prior returns (1 day, 1 week, 1 month, 12 months), volatility compression or expansion, relative volume, gaps, distance from highs.
Catalysts: earnings dates, news, listings, token unlocks.
Structure: market cap, float, short interest, liquidity, sector.
Crypto-specific: funding rates, open interest, exchange flows, social attention.

Then measure:

Lift: P(top gainer | feature) versus the base rate.
Post-event behavior: forward returns at 1, 5, and 20 days after an asset appears on the list, split by liquidity, catalyst type, and market regime.

Model. Start with simple sorts and logistic regression before any machine learning. If an effect isn't visible in a simple decile sort, a neural network is probably "finding" it by overfitting.

3. What is actually "proven"

"Proven" in markets means documented across decades, across markets, and out of sample. Even then, effects decay. McLean and Pontiff (2016) found that anomaly returns fall by roughly half after publication.

Effect	Evidence	Relevance
Volatility clustering	Among the most robust findings in finance	Volatility is far more predictable than direction, which is the basis for TP/SL sizing

Time-series momentum (trend)	Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen (2012), across futures and FX	Works across asset classes

A key takeaway is that the catalyst matters more than the move. A 20% gain on earnings behaves differently from a 20% gain on no news in a low-float stock.

4. Which asset class is more predictable?

Predictability and profitability are different things. Inefficiency lives where trading is expensive, capacity is small, or risk is ugly.

FX majors: the most efficient and the hardest for short-term direction. Costs are low, but so is the edge.
Large-cap equities: very efficient intraday and daily. Factor effects exist at longer horizons.
Small and micro-cap equities: more predictable statistically, but spreads, slippage, and borrow constraints consume the edge.
Crypto: probably the strongest raw momentum and attention effects. It also has regime instability, exchange risk, funding costs, and only about ten years of data.
Mutual funds: they don't fit a TP/SL framework at all, since they are priced once a day at NAV with no intraday execution. Treat them as a separate, slow allocation problem.

Don't assume the ranking; measure it. Run the identical pipeline on each asset class and compare out-of-sample information coefficient and net-of-cost Sharpe. That gives you an apples-to-apples answer for your horizon and cost structure.

5. Symbol versus symbol plus direction

Per-symbol models mostly overfit. One symbol gives you perhaps a few hundred independent observations at a daily horizon. If you test 500 symbols and pick the "most predictable" ones, you are selecting noise.

A better approach is to pool across symbols and let characteristics (liquidity, volatility, sector, market cap) explain the differences. Predictability belongs more to the setup, regime, and asset type than to the ticker. To claim that a particular symbol is predictable, you need it to hold out of sample after multiple-testing correction.

Direction asymmetry is real and worth modeling separately:

Equities have positive drift, so longs have a tailwind.
Shorts face borrow fees, squeezes, and unbounded losses. Fading low-float gainers has statistical support, but the tail risk is brutal and shares are often unborrowable.
In crypto, funding rates create a measurable cost or subsidy for each side.

So test in a hierarchy: asset class, then setup type, then direction, then symbol cluster. Require more evidence at each finer level.

6. TP/SL design

TP/SL does not create edge; it only reshapes the return distribution. Under a random walk, P(hit TP before SL) ≈ SL / (TP + SL), so expectancy is zero before costs and negative after. A 2:1 reward-to-risk ratio sounds good but just means you win about 33% of the time unless you have a real directional edge.

Practical steps:

Scale to volatility. Set levels in ATR or forecast-volatility multiples, not fixed percentages.
Use the triple-barrier method (López de Prado): TP, SL, and a time-out. Label each historical signal by which barrier is hit first.
Analyze MAE/MFE (maximum adverse and favorable excursion) on historical signals to see where stops and targets should sit.
Know your breakeven win rate: (SL + costs) / (TP + SL).
Be conservative in backtests. If TP and SL both fall inside the same bar, assume the SL was hit first.
7. Backtesting: yes, but as a filter

A backtest can reject a bad idea; it cannot prove a good one. The main pitfalls are:

Look-ahead bias, such as using the close to trade at the close.
Survivorship bias.
Unrealistic fills, including gaps through stops, halts, and thin liquidity.
Ignored costs.
Overfitting through repeated trials, which is the biggest of these.

The methods that guard against these:

Time-based splits: train, validation, and an untouched final test set that you look at once.
Walk-forward analysis, or purged k-fold cross-validation with an embargo.
Trial counting: log every variation you test, then apply the deflated Sharpe ratio or White's Reality Check. Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2016) suggest requiring a t-statistic above 3, not 2.
Baselines: compare against random entries with the same TP/SL and holding period, and against buy-and-hold.
Sensitivity checks: the edge should survive doubled costs and small parameter changes. A sharp peak in parameter space is overfitting; a plateau is a good sign.

For sample size, t-stat ≈ Sharpe × √years. A Sharpe of 1 needs about 4 years to reach t = 2 and about 9 years to reach t = 3. For trade-based stats, you want hundreds of independent trades.

8. Forward-testing
Freeze the rules in writing before you start. Any change resets the clock.
Log signals with timestamps before the outcome is known: entry, TP, SL, and size. An immutable log is your pre-registration.
Paper trade with realistic fill assumptions for 1 to 3 months or at least 100 signals.
Go live at small size. This reveals slippage, partial fills, and your own behavior, which paper trading hides.
Compare live results to the backtest distribution. Expect a 30–50% haircut on backtest performance.
Predefine kill criteria, such as a drawdown beyond the 95th percentile of backtest simulations, or expectancy statistically below zero after N trades.

This is a big question, so I'll treat it as a research program rather than a single answer. The short version: direction is weakly predictable, volatility is strongly predictable, and the ranking of assets relative to each other is more predictable than the absolute path of any one asset. Nearly everything below follows from that.

1. Reframe the problem before touching data

"Top gainers" lists are the output of a process. Reverse-engineering them means asking: what was observable at time t-1 that was over-represented among the names that showed up on the list at time t? That's a cross-sectional ranking problem, and it is a fundamentally different (and easier) problem than "will BTC go up tomorrow?"

Three distinct prediction tasks hide inside your question:

Task	Question	Type	Typical difficulty
A. Selection	Which symbols will be in the top-N movers next period?	Cross-sectional ranking / rare-event classification	Moderate
B. Direction	Will symbol X go up or down over horizon H?	Binary / ternary classification per symbol	Hard
C. Path	Given a long entry, will price hit +TP before -SL within H bars?	Barrier / survival problem	Hard, but tradeable

Most people jump straight to B. You should build A first (it's where the real edge in "top gainers" lives), use C as your label definition, and treat B as a derived quantity.

A warning about the target itself: a top-gainers list is drenched in selection bias. The names on it are, by construction, the extremes of a fat-tailed distribution. If you study only the names that made the list, you'll "discover" that high relative volume, small float, and news catalysts predict big moves — but you'll never see the thousands of names with the same features that went nowhere. You must always build the dataset from the full universe, including the losers and the non-movers.

2. Define the label precisely (this is 50% of the work)

A vague target produces a vague model. For each symbol and each timestamp, define:

Horizon H: 1 day, 5 days, 1 hour, etc. Pick a few and test them separately; predictability changes drastically with horizon.
Label for Task A: 1 if the forward return ranks in the top k% of the universe at t+H, else 0. Or use the continuous rank percentile (better for ranking models).
Label for Task C (triple-barrier labeling, from López de Prado): for each potential entry, set an upper barrier (TP), a lower barrier (SL), and a vertical barrier (time out). Label +1, -1, or 0 depending on which barrier is touched first. Make TP/SL multiples of recent volatility (ATR or realized σ), not fixed percentages, or your labels will be dominated by high-vol names.

This does two things: your model is now trained on the exact object you will trade, and your TP/SL are baked into the research rather than bolted on afterward.

Don't take anyone's word on this; measure it, and measure it per horizon and per regime. The tools:

Statistical predictability tests (model-free)

Autocorrelation of returns at lags 1..k. Positive → momentum/continuation; negative → mean reversion. Equity indices at daily horizons are near zero; individual small caps show negative lag-1 (reversal); crypto shows mild positive at weekly horizons during trends.
Variance ratio test (Lo–MacKinlay): tests whether log price behaves like a random walk. VR > 1 at horizon k means trending; VR < 1 means mean-reverting.
Hurst exponent (rolling): ~0.5 random, >0.5 persistent, <0.5 anti-persistent. Noisy on short windows; use it as a regime descriptor, not a signal.
Autocorrelation of absolute or squared returns: this is where predictability is strong across all asset classes. Volatility clusters. This is why GARCH-family and HAR-RV models work and directional models mostly don't.

Model-based predictability (the honest version)

Fit the same simple model (e.g., gradient boosting on 20 standard features) across each asset class with identical walk-forward protocol, then compare out-of-sample Information Coefficient (Spearman rank correlation between predicted and realized returns, per period). An IC of 0.02–0.05 is realistic and tradeable in equities cross-sections; 0.10+ sustained is suspicious.
IC Information Ratio (mean IC / std IC) tells you how stable the edge is, which matters more than its average.

What the literature and practice fairly consistently show (the "proven" part, with the caveat that effects decay as they become known):

Equities: cross-sectional momentum (12-1 month), short-term reversal (1 week to 1 month, strongest in small caps), post-earnings-announcement drift, low-volatility anomaly, and short-term continuation after high-volume breakouts in large caps. Small-cap "top gainers" on no fundamental news mean-revert with high probability — this is one of the most robust findings and is directly relevant to your target. Large-cap gainers on earnings beats tend to drift further.
Crypto: time-series momentum (weekly/monthly) has held up; funding-rate extremes and perpetual basis predict short-term reversals; exchange netflows and stablecoin flows have modest predictive power. Lag-1 daily reversal is weak. Correlation to BTC is so high that most alt "predictability" is just BTC beta — strip it out before you claim anything.
Forex: carry (interest-rate differential) and trend are the two durable effects; short-horizon direction is close to unpredictable outside macro event windows. Spreads are tiny, so FX rewards high-frequency microstructure edges that retail generally can't access.
Mutual funds: essentially not a trading target. They're priced once daily at NAV, you can't short them, settlement is T+1/T+2, and frequent trading is penalized. Their "top gainers" are just the factor/sector exposure of the underlying holdings. If you want that exposure, trade the ETF or the constituents. Useful for research (fund flows predict factor crowding), useless for signals with TP/SL.

My honest ranking of practical directional-signal tractability for a solo/small team: equities cross-section > crypto (because of inefficiencies and funding/flow data, offset by exchange risk and costs) > FX > mutual funds. For volatility prediction, all of the first three are good.

4. Symbol vs. symbol + direction: how to think about it

Per-symbol predictability is mostly an illusion. If you compute a rolling IC for a single symbol, you'll find periods where it looks great, but the sample is tiny and the result is unstable. The right structure is a pooled model across the universe with symbol-level features (liquidity, sector, volatility bucket, market cap, exchange) so the model can learn "names like this behave like that" instead of "AAPL behaves like this."

Direction is asymmetric, and you should model it that way.

Downside moves are faster and more volatile ("leverage effect"); upside grinds. This means an identical TP/SL ratio is not equally appropriate for longs and shorts. Shorts often want tighter time barriers and wider stops.
Costs are asymmetric: shorting stocks involves borrow fees and recall risk; shorting crypto on perps involves funding (which can be in your favor or against you); FX is symmetric.
Many anomalies are one-sided. Small-cap gainer reversal is a short edge; earnings drift is mostly a long edge; momentum crashes hit the short leg.

Practical approach: train a ternary triple-barrier model (long wins / short wins / neither), then evaluate the long and short predictions as separate strategies with separate cost models. You'll typically find one side is tradeable and the other isn't for a given class. Report them separately; never let a strong long side mask a losing short side in a combined Sharpe.

Also worth separating: "when" vs. "which." A model that knows which names will outperform but not when the market moves is a market-neutral long/short strategy. A model that knows when but not which is a market-timing strategy. These have very different capacity, costs, and drawdown profiles. Decompose your returns into market/beta, sector, and idiosyncratic components so you know which one you actually have.

5. Actually reverse-engineering the top-gainers list

Concrete workflow:

Reconstruct the lists yourself from historical OHLCV over the full point-in-time universe (including delisted names — survivorship bias alone will fabricate a 2–4%/yr edge). Don't scrape published lists; they're inconsistent and incomplete.
Build features as of the close before the gainer day (and 5, 20 days before). Candidates that recur in the literature and in practice:
Relative volume (today's volume / 20-day average), especially on the prior day
Gap size at open and whether the gap filled
Float, market cap, dollar volume (liquidity bucket)
Short interest and days-to-cover (squeeze fuel)
Distance from 52-week high/low; recent drawdown
Realized volatility and its change (vol expansion precedes breakouts)
Scheduled catalysts: earnings date, FDA dates, index inclusion, token unlocks, exchange listings
News/sentiment count and novelty (first mention vs. repeat)
Sector/theme momentum (gainers cluster: when one uranium name runs, others follow)
Crypto-specific: funding rate, open-interest change, perp basis, exchange inflows, social volume spikes
Options-specific: unusual call volume, IV skew changes
Fit a classifier for P(in top-N tomorrow). Base rate is tiny (if N=20 out of 5,000, that's 0.4%), so accuracy is meaningless. Use precision@k, lift over base rate, AUC-PR, and calibration (Brier score, reliability plots).
Study what happens after the list. This is the actually tradeable part. Condition on being a top gainer plus features (cap, catalyst type, volume, close location in the day's range) and measure forward returns at 1, 3, 5, 20 days with their MAE/MFE distributions. You'll find distinct clusters: "news-driven large-cap continuation," "no-news small-cap fade," "crypto listing pump and dump." Each gets its own TP/SL geometry.

The uncomfortable truth: predicting who will be a top gainer tomorrow from public data is hard and low-precision. Predicting what a top gainer does next is much more tractable and is where most "momentum-scanner" strategies actually make money.

6. Designing TP/SL properly

Don't optimize TP and SL as free parameters — that's the fastest route to overfitting. Instead:

Express everything in volatility units (e.g., TP = 2.0 × ATR₁₄, SL = 1.0 × ATR₁₄). Volatility is predictable, so this adapts automatically and keeps risk per trade comparable across names.
Use MAE/MFE analysis on your historical signal set: plot the maximum favorable and maximum adverse excursion of each trade. The SL should sit just beyond where winning trades typically bottom (so you don't stop out of winners); the TP should sit near where favorable excursion stops growing. This gives you a data-driven geometry with one or two degrees of freedom rather than a grid search.
Compute expectancy explicitly:
E = p × TP − (1−p) × SL − costs, with the breakeven hit rate p* = (SL + costs) / (TP + SL). A 2:1 reward/risk needs ~34% wins plus costs; 1:2 needs ~67%. Check that your model's calibrated probability clears the breakeven with margin.
Include a time stop. Signals decay; a trade that hasn't resolved in H bars has usually lost its thesis. The triple barrier does this for you.
Consider trailing stops only after measuring them; they often lower hit rate and raise payoff ratio, and the net effect is strategy-specific.
Size by volatility and cap by fractional Kelly (quarter-Kelly is common). The TP/SL defines the per-trade payoff; sizing defines whether you survive the sequence.
7. Backtesting without lying to yourself

The pitfalls, in order of how often they ruin results:

Look-ahead bias: using the close to decide a trade and filling at that same close; using fundamentals or revised data before they were published. Fill at the next bar's open (or worse). Use point-in-time databases.
Survivorship bias: discussed above. Non-negotiable.
Cost realism: spread, commissions, slippage as a function of your order size vs. ADV, borrow fees for shorts, funding for perps, exchange withdrawal/transfer frictions in crypto. For small caps and alts, slippage often exceeds the entire gross edge. Model it as a function of liquidity, not a flat bps number.
Data snooping / multiple testing: if you try 200 feature combinations and horizons, the best one will look great by chance. Use the Deflated Sharpe Ratio or the Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO) via combinatorial purged cross-validation. Keep a log of every test you run — the number of trials is a parameter in the significance test.
Leakage across folds: overlapping labels (a 5-day forward return at t shares information with t+1). Use purged k-fold with an embargo or strict walk-forward (train on [0, T], test on [T, T+Δ], roll). Never shuffle time series randomly.
Regime concentration: a strategy whose entire P&L came from March 2020 or the 2021 crypto bull is a bet on that regime recurring. Report P&L by year and by volatility regime.
Too few trades: with fewer than a few hundred independent trades, your Sharpe confidence interval is wider than the Sharpe itself. Compute the Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio or minimum track record length.

Always benchmark against naive baselines: buy-and-hold, random entries with the same TP/SL geometry and holding period, and a simple momentum or reversal rule. If your model doesn't beat random entries with the same exit logic, your "edge" is in the exits or in the asset's drift, not in the signal.

Run Monte Carlo on the trade sequence (bootstrap or shuffle trade order) to get drawdown distributions rather than one historical path. The historical max drawdown is one draw from a distribution; you want the 95th percentile.

8. Forward testing: the only test that counts

Backtests tell you a hypothesis is not obviously wrong. Forward tests tell you whether it survives contact with reality.

Stage 1 — Shadow/paper trading with a live feed. Generate signals in real time, log them before the outcome is known (timestamped, immutable), and compute hypothetical fills using actual quotes. This catches data-feed differences, timing bugs, and the gap between "close price" and "price you could have gotten." Expect your paper results to be worse than your backtest. If they're better, something is wrong.

Stage 2 — Small live capital. Size so that a 100% strategy loss is a nuisance. The purpose is to measure implementation shortfall: realized fill price vs. signal price, partial fills, rejected orders, exchange outages, borrow unavailability. Track live vs. paper vs. backtest for the same signals side by side.

Stage 3 — Scale with decay monitoring. Alpha decays. Use a CUSUM or sequential probability ratio test on your live hit rate or per-trade expectancy against the backtest expectation. Pre-define the kill criterion (e.g., "if the 95% lower confidence bound on live expectancy falls below zero after N trades, stop"). Define it before you start so you don't rationalize.

How long is enough? Roughly: to distinguish a true Sharpe of 1.0 from 0 at 95% confidence, you need about 4 years of daily data (because SR standard error ≈ 1/√T in years, roughly). You cannot shortcut this with calendar time, but you can with trade count: a cross-sectional strategy making 50 independent trades a day accumulates statistical evidence far faster than one making 50 a year. This is a strong argument for favoring high-breadth, lower-conviction systems over single-symbol sniping — the Fundamental Law of Active Management (IR ≈ IC × √breadth) makes this explicit.

Also run pre-registration on yourself: write down the hypothesis, the universe, the horizon, the expected hit rate and payoff, and the kill rule, then don't touch the strategy during the test window. Anything you change resets the clock.

9. The metrics dashboard

Signal quality (model level)

Information Coefficient (Spearman), its mean and its IR (mean/std), by period and by regime
Precision@k, lift over base rate, AUC-PR for rare-event targets
Brier score and calibration curve (are your 60% calls actually right 60% of the time?)
Decile/quintile spread: average forward return of top-decile predictions minus bottom-decile
Signal decay curve: IC as a function of lag between signal and execution

Trade quality (strategy level)

Hit rate, average win, average loss, payoff ratio, expectancy per trade (in volatility units and currency)
Profit factor (gross wins / gross losses)
MAE/MFE distributions
Average holding time and fraction of trades resolved by TP, SL, and time-stop
Turnover and cost ratio (total costs / gross P&L) — if above ~50%, the strategy is fragile

Portfolio quality

Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar; Deflated Sharpe or PSR given the number of trials
Max drawdown and its duration; Monte Carlo drawdown percentiles
Skew and tail ratio (are you selling insurance without knowing it?)
Beta and correlation to the underlying market (how much is just BTC/SPY exposure?)
Return attribution: market, sector/factor, idiosyncratic
Capacity estimate: at what AUM does slippage eat the edge?

Live-vs-sim health

Implementation shortfall (bps per trade)
Live hit rate / expectancy vs. backtest with running confidence intervals
CUSUM alarm status
Data-quality checks (missing bars, stale quotes, outliers) — bad data is a more common cause of fake signals than bad models
10. A prioritized build order
Data layer: point-in-time universe, OHLCV, corporate actions, delistings; for crypto, funding, OI, basis; for equities, earnings dates and short interest. Store raw and derived separately, with timestamps of availability.
Labeling: triple-barrier with vol-scaled barriers at 2–3 horizons.
Baselines: random-entry-same-exit, buy-and-hold, simple momentum and reversal. These are your null hypotheses.
Predictability survey: variance ratios, autocorrelation, pooled-model IC per asset class and horizon. This tells you where to spend effort.
Top-gainer feature study: build the list from the full universe, characterize pre-conditions and post-list behavior by cluster.
Model: gradient boosting (LightGBM/XGBoost) with purged walk-forward CV; it handles mixed features and nonlinearity and is interpretable via SHAP. Neural nets rarely earn their complexity here.
Backtest with full cost model, multiple-testing correction, Monte Carlo.
Paper → small live → scale, with pre-registered kill criteria.
Conclusion

Reverse-engineering top gainers is really two problems wearing one costume. The glamorous one — guessing which obscure name will rip tomorrow — is dominated by noise, selection bias, and catalysts that aren't in your data. The unglamorous one — characterizing what kinds of big movers continue versus fade, in which asset class, on which side, with what volatility-scaled TP/SL — is well supported by decades of evidence and is what working systems actually exploit. Predictability lives in the cross-section and in volatility far more than in the directional path of any single symbol; longs and shorts are different strategies and must be judged separately; mutual funds belong in the research pile, not the trading pile. Prove it with purged walk-forward backtests against random-entry baselines, correct for how many things you tried, then earn the right to believe it through logged, pre-registered forward tests measured on expectancy, calibration, cost ratio, and live-versus-sim shortfall. If the edge survives that gauntlet, it's real; if it only survives the backtest, it was never there.

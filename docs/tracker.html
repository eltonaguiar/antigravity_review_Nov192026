Executive summary

Every pick this estate publishes — the harvest consensus, the per-class "best candidate" table and each agent's ranked list — was previously argued about and then forgotten. Nothing measured what happened next. This tracker makes a pick an append-only record with a timestamp, an owner, a cost basis and a benchmark, so the question "what if we had actually taken that?" is answered with data instead of opinion.

Three things were built. A ledger (picktrack_picks) that is immutable: what a pick claimed lives in one table, what it returned lives in another, so rescoring can never rewrite history. A scoring rule frozen before results existed: entry at the first open strictly after publication, per-row round-turn cost, benchmark measured over the same window with the same rule. And a rating design that refuses to collapse to one number, because a high hit rate with small wins and a low hit rate with large wins are different products rather than better and worse ones.

Plain English — what the numbers mean

Every term on this page, decoded. If a number cannot be explained in one sentence, it should not be on the page.

TermWhat it actually means
bp (basis point)0.01 of one percent. 100 bp = 1%. So −12.7 bp is a loss of 0.127% — on $10,000 that is −$12.70.
net / grossNet = after trading fees, what you actually keep. Gross = before fees, the flattering number.
long / shortLong = you make money if the price goes up. Short = you make money if it goes down.
excess / versus benchmarkYour return minus the return of simply holding the benchmark. Losing 1% while the market loses 3% is +2% excess — a loss, but a better loss. This is the only honest way to compare picks, because in a rising market everything looks smart.
mark-to-marketValued at today's price without selling. A snapshot, not a result.
day-zeroThe pick was published today and a few hours have passed. There is no track record yet — only a starting point.
pending_entryThe market has not opened since we published, so the clock has not started. We show no number rather than invent a zero.
nHow many trades are in the record. A 90% win rate on 10 trades means almost nothing; on 500 it means something.
Wilson intervalThe honest range around a win rate given how few trades there are. 97.6% on a small sample might really be 86% — or 99.6%.
expectancyAverage profit per trade. Positive means the method makes money over many trades.
worst-case expectancyThe same average, recomputed assuming your loss rate is as bad as the data still allows. If this is negative, a good-looking record may just be luck.
profit factorTotal winnings ÷ total losses. 2.0 means you win twice as much as you lose.
INSUFFICIENT_NToo few trades to judge. Not a bad rating — a refusal to rate.

Worked example, in one sentence

The ENJ and AUDJPY rows

AUDJPY is the Australian dollar against the Japanese yen. We bet it would rise. It fell, and after fees the loss was 0.127% (−12.7 bp). ENJ is a crypto coin called Enjin; we bet it would rise and it fell 1.21% after fees (−121.2 bp). But over those same hours Bitcoin fell about 3.07%, so compared with simply holding Bitcoin we were 1.85% ahead (+185.4 bp). Both were losses; one was a much better loss than the market's. Both are day-zero snapshots, not results.

Sample size: the finding that matters most

Assumptions, stated because they were never supplied. The only inputs actually given were two headline hit rates — 97.6% and 49.4% — plus the fact that the first trades with a sub-one-week horizon. Sample sizes (41 and 500) and the 22:1 loss-to-win ratio are illustrative placeholders, not measurements. The arithmetic below is correct for those assumptions and is presented as a worked illustration, not as a finding about any real person. This is the same defect class as a stale rule: internally consistent numbers built on an input nobody checked.

How many consecutive wins a record needs before its 95% upper bound on the loss rate falls below its break-even loss rate. Below that count, a good-looking record is statistically indistinguishable from a bad one.

Ranking rule — why we do not sort by the headline number

Sorting by point expectancy ranks the wrong candidate first. Peer review produced this case, and it reproduces:

CandidatenWin ratePoint expectancyExpectancy at worst-case loss rate
TAIL — high hit rate, rare huge losses4197.6%+53.66 bp−129.16 bp
BORING — mediocre but stable40052.5%+36.25 bp+26.46 bp

Both look strong on the headline. Only one survives its own confidence interval. So the ordering key is the worst-case expectancy, never the point estimate, and a candidate whose worst case is negative is not rankable at all however good the headline looks.

When a pick becomes reportable

A pick earns a verdict only when all four hold: n reaches its pick-type minimum (day trade 100, swing 60, position 40, long-term 20, buy-and-hold 10) · expectancy confidence interval excludes zero after multiple-testing correction · the sign repeats across at least two out-of-sample periods · and expectancy at the upper bound of the loss rate stays positive, not the observed rate.

Ratings are per asset class and holding period. A crypto scalper and an equity swing trader averaged together describe nobody, so no cohort is ever pooled.

What cannot be back-tested. Anything needing more than four monthly periods — the consensus feed's entire history. Scoring today's consensus against past prices would be look-ahead and would produce a flattering, meaningless number. That is precisely why this tracker is forward only — the honest alternative to a fake backtest is a record that starts now and accrues.

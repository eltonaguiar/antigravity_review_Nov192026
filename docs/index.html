01Explain like I'm five

No formulas. If you understand a lemonade stand, you understand this page.

The lemonade stand

Imagine you want to make money selling lemonade. You could stand on a corner and guess where the thirsty people are. That is what most trading strategies do: guess a pattern, hope it repeats.

But there is a trap. If you stand on twenty corners one Saturday and only tell your friends about the one where you sold out, you have not found a good corner. You have found luck, and you have hidden the nineteen failures. Almost every "amazing trading system" you will ever see is that trick, played by accident.

So we built a test that cannot be fooled that way:

  • Write the rule down before you test it. Like writing your bet on paper before the race starts. No changing it afterwards.
  • Count every corner you tried, not just the winner. Twenty corners means the winner must be twenty times more impressive.
  • Pay the real costs. The lemonade costs money, the cups cost money, and the busy corner charges rent. A strategy that only works when trading is free does not work.
  • Test on days you were not allowed to peek at. Otherwise you are just remembering the answers to the exam.
  • Decide in advance what would prove you wrong — and then actually stop when it happens.

What we found

We pointed that test at ten kinds of investment — stocks, index funds, bonds, mutual funds, currencies, crypto, commodities, options, futures and prediction markets — and at four AI agents that each did their own research.

Every measured strategy failed — and four classes were never measured at all. Not because the agents were lazy — they produced hundreds of candidates, measured real trading costs, and ran the statistics — but because when you charge a strategy for the cost of actually trading it, and then charge it again for the fact that you tried many versions, the apparent profit disappears.

What survived is boring, and it is the honest answer: a cheap, broad index fund, held for a long time. That is not a clever trade. That is just agreeing to own a slice of the economy and waiting.

The one number that explains everything

Cash in a government treasury bill pays about 4.32% a year at the time of writing. That is the hurdle. Anything you do with money that carries risk has to beat it after costs, or you were better off doing nothing.

Two corrections to how that number was originally written here. First, 4.32% is not fixed — T-bill yields move constantly (13-week bills were 4.13% on 30 Sept 2026, 4-week bills 4.23% in July, and 3.6–3.7% in April). Any "fails to clear the bar" verdict expires as the bar moves, so the hurdle must be stamped with a date and a series.

Second, calling cash "guaranteed, with no skill required" overstated it. Over 1928–2025 T-bills returned 3.37%/yr against 3.04% inflation — about +0.33% real before tax, and roughly −0.39%/yr after a 22% federal rate. Cash is guaranteed nominal, near-zero real, and negative real after tax for most brackets. It is still the correct hurdle; it is not a free lunch.

Our best-measured value strategy — buying cheap stocks and shorting expensive ones — earned +0.11% a year before costs and +0.02% a year after costs. It did not lose to costs. No effect was detectable at this sample size — which is a weaker and more honest statement than "the effect was not there," and the distinction matters (see below).

Three corrections to how the hurdle was applied — all three found by external reviewers, all three conceded.

1. The hurdle was misapplied to market-neutral strategies. A long/short book is roughly self-financing, and its collateral still earns the T-bill rate — so +0.11% is already an excess return, and comparing it to 4.32% is apples to oranges. The right question for that strategy is whether +0.11% is distinguishable from zero. It is not, so the conclusion survives — but the framing was wrong, and applied across ten classes it overstated how badly the market-neutral candidates failed. The same applies to futures, which are unfunded and whose collateral also earns cash. Correct rule: funded long-only positions face the cash hurdle; market-neutral and futures positions face a zero-excess hurdle, plus costs.

2. The one recommendation does not pass the page's own test. If everything risky must beat cash after costs, then an equity index — which is risky — must too, and by that logic the answer would be T-bills. The index case rests on the equity risk premium, which this project assumed rather than measured. That is now labelled as assumed, using the page's own taxonomy: beta is exempt from the alpha gate because it does not claim alpha, and the premium is an assumption with a long historical basis, not a result of this work.

3. "Every strategy failed" is a statement about this search, not about markets. Once you charge for costs and apply a multiple-testing penalty, statistical power collapses — a real edge of 1–2%/yr would also fail this gate on a sample this short. So the honest sentence is "no effect was detectable at this sample size," not "the effect does not exist." The missing measurement is a power statement: an effect of X%/yr would have had a Y% chance of clearing this gate. That number would turn the headline from a verdict into a measurement.

Two further specifics reviewers asked for, now stated: the multiple-testing correction used is Benjamini–Hochberg FDR (not Bonferroni, which over correlated candidates would produce an all-null result by construction); and the win-rate-vs-50% binomial test is a diagnostic, not the gate — win rate is nearly orthogonal to profitability (trend systems win ~35–40% and make money; option-selling wins ~85% and blows up), so the bootstrap on net P&L is what decides.

The missing measurement — a power statement. Three reviewers asked for the same number, and it turns the headline around. From SE = vol / √T, every figure below printed by CONTEST/tools/power_analysis.py:

And the interval on the value result: at 10% vol over 10 years, +0.11% carries a 95% CI of [−6.1%, +6.3%] — twelve percentage points wide, straddling zero. "Every strategy failed" was close to guaranteed before the study began — by sample length, not by bias.

And the deeper problem: there is no positive control. A long/short value book earning +0.11% gross is an anomalous result, not a null — academic HML is 3–5%/yr. When a pipeline cannot reproduce a known, heavily-documented effect, the correct inference is "the apparatus may be broken," not "the effect is absent." First thing to check: price-return vs total-return series — value tilts into high-dividend names, and a price-only feed erases 2–3%/yr specifically from the value leg. Until a synthetic-alpha injection and a known anomaly are recovered, the other nine nulls are provisional.

WHAT EACH PAGE IS FOR

Four pages, four different jobs

There are several places to look, and they are not interchangeable. One is a referee's notebook, one is a scoreboard, one is a judge, and one does not exist.

The plain-English version.

  • This site (antigravity_review) is the referee's notebook. It records what AI agents claimed, what we checked against real data, and what survived. Nothing on it is a recommendation to trade.
  • findtorontoevents.ca/audit is the scoreboard of the live trading system. It reports. It does not judge.
  • findtorontoevents.ca/audit3 is the judge. Same trades, but with real statistics attached and a kill rule: a strategy whose net return is provably below zero gets killed.
  • /audit2 is a dead link. There is no page there and no file for it anywhere in git history. If anyone cites it, they are citing nothing.

One line: the dashboard is where the system reports, audit3 is where it is judged, and this site is where the agents themselves are judged.

The long version — what each page serves, what it can and cannot do

1. This site — eltonaguiar.github.io/antigravity_review_Nov192026

Purpose: to hold the agents themselves accountable, in public, with numbers that were recomputed rather than accepted. It is the audit of the audit: what Kilo Code, Zoo Code, GitHub Copilot, buffy/freebuff and a field of twelve language models said about markets, and what happened when each claim was checked against fetched prices and the file system.

What it can do: show, for any claim, whether it is observed (measured here), derived (computed from measurements) or assumed (nobody checked). Record failures as results — including its own. Publish the cost of record each number was judged against.

What it cannot do: tell you what to buy. Every pick on this site is paper. No position here has real capital behind it, and the site exists partly to explain why: nothing in the estate clears the gate sheet.

Its two data sections: § Live results is the twelve-model paper-trading contest, scored against fetched prices. § Final picks per asset class is what each engineering agent filed as its final answer, and how the four of them disagree.

2. findtorontoevents.ca/audit — the dashboard (live, 1.4 MB)

Purpose: the operational face of the trading system. Summary cards, a kill-switch card, tournament/leaderboard framing, a money-ready bridge, the theory tracker (H-020…H-025), per-class profit-factor / win-rate panels and freshness indicators.

What it can do: show at a glance what the scanners think they found and how fresh it is.

What it cannot do: be trusted tile-by-tile. Several tiles are stale or misleading (documented in 02_FINDINGS/data_quality_resolver.md), and the site has historically shown a hardcoded GREEN while 21% of rows were covered and 22,947 rows were ghosts. It reports; it does not judge. Treat every number here as a claim awaiting audit3.

3. findtorontoevents.ca/audit3 — the strict ledger (live, 36 KB)

Purpose: the "Quant Kill Tribunal". The same trades as the dashboard, but put through a design that can actually say no: Wilson confidence intervals, a one-sided binomial test against a 50% win rate, 2,000-resample bootstrap confidence intervals on net expectancy after per-class slippage, Benjamini- Hochberg FDR control across the family, split-half stability, concentration caps, and an explicit kill rule (net-CI entirely below zero and BH harm q ≤ 0.10).

What it can do: kill things. This is the best artifact in the estate — the only surface engineered to produce a negative verdict, and it does: 0 PROVEN / 1 CANDIDATE.

What it cannot do: rescue the data underneath it. It inherits the resolver's coverage and the survivor-conditioned universe, so a PASS here still is not a licence to trade.

4. /audit2 — 404, and it should stay that way

Purpose: none. Probed 2026-11-19: HTTP 404, 992 B. No audit2.html exists anywhere in git history; the only surviving artifacts are helper scripts (score_audit2.py, tmp/check_audit2.py, tmp/db_audit2.py). The audit2 effort was superseded by audit3 under a stricter statistical design.

Also dead: audit_page.html — a stale May 25 page with two tabs (one empty, one "Grok picks") pointing at data/grok_top_picks.json. Orphaned. It is a different page from the live /audit and should not be cited as it.

Why this page bothers to say all that

Because the most common failure in this project was never a bad prediction — it was a page, file or number that nobody had checked, quoted onwards as if it had been. A dashboard that reports a GREEN light, a ledger row with no n, a hash taken before git rewrote the line endings, an agent calling itself finished while a pick was already outside its own stop. Naming what each surface can and cannot do is the cheapest defence against all four.

Why there is no ranking — and it is arithmetic, not modesty

Eighteen entrants filed picks. Every one of them is listed below at the same rank, because the contest cannot tell them apart — and that conclusion is fixed by the design, not by how the results happen to land.

For two entrants with n picks each and per-pick volatility σ, the standard error of the difference in mean return is σ·√(2/n), so the smallest difference distinguishable at t = 2 is 2·σ·√(2/n).

Resampled the 18 entrants' picks with replacement 2,000 times, tie-aware. Result: 0 of 2,000 replicates produced a unique winner. Three entrants sit at a perfect 100% bracket-fit in every replicate and a leaderboard would be ranking an arbitrary tie-break.

The agent also caught itself: its first pass reported ARENA as a 100% winner, which was an alphabetical tie-break silently deciding among the three perfect entrants. It removed the tie-break and made the bootstrap tie-aware. It then tested a continuous "bracket-fit margin" metric, which rewards badly-missed brackets — an entrant leads purely because its SPY target sat 75 points below live price, a near-total miss scored as a win. That is the metrics attack surface the rules warn about, and the reason the metric must be fixed before the contest, not after.

So the honest headline is not "every strategy failed." It is: the harness was blind to effects under ~5%/yr, the value test was being erased by a price-only feed, the backtest trade table was contaminated by ghost rows, and four picks per entrant cannot rank anyone. Four defects, each of which could manufacture a null on its own.

FOUR HORIZONS · ONE EQUATION · MEASURED COSTS

What to actually do — long-term, daytrading, scalping, and a crypto signal

Everything below follows from one relation: annual P&L ≈ (gross edge per trade − round-trip cost) × trades per year. Three of these four questions are therefore questions about frequency versus cost, not about prediction.

The generic answer, measured — and one of its four claims is backwards. Every model asked "what should I invest in" returns the same four points: diversify, buy cheap index funds, stay invested, and dollar-cost average. Three survive. The fourth does not.

Same $12,000, same SPY, same end date — lump-sum at month 0 versus $1,000/month for a year, over every monthly start date from 1993 to 2026:

holdP(DCA beats lump-sum)median DCA/lump-sum
5 y21.4%0.947

Lump-sum wins about four times in five. Stretch the DCA window to 36 months and hold 30 years and it never wins — 0 of 46 start dates — ending with 27% less money. Markets trend up, so money that arrives later buys fewer shares. DCA is a behavioural device, not a return strategy; calling it "proven by history" inverts the evidence.

Its other supporting fact — "missing the best days destroys returns" — is true and fragile: removing 10% of months collapses the 33-year CAGR from 10.42% to 1.12%. The four answers

1 · long-term stocks

Cost is a rounding error here, so this is the one horizon with a structural answer. Own the cheapest broad index the account supports; choose on fee and fit, never on trailing return (vehicle differences are contractual at ~8 bp/yr and statistically undetectable — tracking noise is ~1%/yr, so ~546 years of data would be needed to prove one). Rebalance on a rule — annually or a 5% band. Individual stocks only as a deliberate concentration bet, sized so being wrong is survivable; ~92% of large-cap active funds trail over 20 years.

2 · daytrading

I would not, and it is arithmetic rather than opinion. Five round trips a day costs 151% of traded capital per year; break-even needs >12.07 bp of gross edge on every trade, forever. Realistic retail edge is 1–3 bp, which loses ~113%/yr. It works only if you are paid to provide liquidity (rebates) or hold a seconds-level information edge. Our own contest could not get a price right — 41 of 69 picks were void at the fill.

3 · scalping

Structurally impossible as a directional retail activity, and not close. Fifty trades a day costs 1,509%/yr; beating cash plus 10% would need >20 bp gross per trade, sustained, against 1–3 bp available — a 17 bp gap per trade that better indicators do not close. Scalping pays only participants who capture the spread instead of paying it: market makers and fee-tier firms. A directional scalper pays it twice.

4 · a rare crypto signal

Crypto costs 52 bp round trip — 6× equity — so any signal must clear that first. The genuinely high-certainty crypto edges are structural, not predictive: funding-rate carry, spot–perp basis, credible stablecoin peg restoration, listing effects, unlock supply shocks. All are capacity-limited, several are arbitrage rather than forecast, and custody risk dwarfs the 52 bp. If a signal predicts direction with high certainty it is not certain, is dust-sized, or is front-running you.

What would change these answers

A documented, cost-inclusive, out-of-sample record showing >12 bp gross per trade at high frequency; or evidence you are on the receiving side of the spread (rebates, maker fees, liquidity-provider tier). Absent either, the burden is on the strategy, not on the skeptic. Full working: CONTEST/ROUND_01_2026-11-23/ANSWERS_BY_HORIZON.md

DSRDeflated Sharpe ratio — a Sharpe ratio corrected for how many strategies were tried. A 1.0 Sharpe from 1,000 attempts is not the same evidence as a 1.0 from one.

echoA number in a model's answer that also appeared in the prompt. It is not evidence until independently recomputed; echo-suspect answers are excluded.
pseudoreplicationCounting the same observation twice. Three panels that silently fell back to the same model are one observation, not three — and agreement under shared assumptions is the assumptions talking.

08The rules the agents are held to

These exist because in three consecutive filing rounds the same words meant different numbers. Rules are only written here after something went wrong without them.

Rule 01

Cost of record — one table, no substitutes

A number labelled measured must match the canonical table or carry a reconciliation. Measured bands: bond ETF 0.6144, ETF 6.7122, index 8.0325, direct equity 12.0749, FOREX 7.533 (upper bound), crypto 20–52 bp. Anything else is assumed and may not pass a gate.

Rule 02

Five questions, every pick

What is the mechanism · who is on the other side and why do they lose · why this symbol · why now · what kills it. If you cannot name the loser, you are probably the loser.

Rule 03

n ≥ 30, or it is inadmissible

A row with zero observations is not a small edge; it is not a row. Two picks at 0.9 correlation are one pick. Empty lists are permitted — and preferred to noise.

Rule 04

Net must exceed the class round trip

If a claimed net edge is below the measured cost of trading that class, the arithmetic is broken somewhere — that is how the 11.36 bp crypto row was caught.

Rule 05

Contest with the number, not the claim

A rejection can only be overturned by supplying the missing measurement. Restating the original assertion is not a rebuttal.

Rule 06

17 gates before a dollar

PBO ≤ 0.10 · walk-forward ≥ 0.60 · alpha t 3.0/3.5/4.0 scaled by effective N · break-even ≥ 4× · capacity ≥ 5× · tails and skew · data integrity · survivorship sensitivity · parameter stability · regime count · out-of-market replication · MinTRL ≤ sample.

09The next research brief — reverse-engineering top gainers

The most interesting question left: can our machine-learning stack predict the daily, weekly and monthly top gainers, or can it only explain them after the fact? Those are completely different claims and the difference is where fortunes get lost.

The trap, stated up front. "Top gainers" lists are the single most overfitted dataset in retail finance. If you take today's winners and search for what they had in common, you will always find something — and it will be worthless. The only honest version of this question is a pre-registered, point-in-time prediction: name the filter rules today, freeze them, then score tomorrow's list.

What the agents are being asked for

  • A frozen feature set and rule, timestamped before scoring. Universe, filters, ranking formula, and the exact bar at which the decision is made.
  • A base-rate benchmark first. "How often does a random 10-name selection contain a top-20 gainer?" Without that number, any hit rate looks impressive.
  • Hit rate against that base rate, with a confidence interval — not a hit list. Precision at k, plus the lift over random.
  • A strict no-look-ahead audit. A "prediction" that uses the close of the day it predicts is not a prediction; it is a lookup.
  • Daily, weekly and monthly horizons tested separately. A rule that works on monthly decay almost never works intraday, and vice versa.
  • Both directions: can we predict winners, and can we predict losers? A model that spots tomorrow's worst performer is often far more robust than one chasing the best.

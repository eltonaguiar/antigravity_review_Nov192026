01Explain like I'm five

No formulas. If you understand a lemonade stand, you understand this page.

The lemonade stand

Imagine you want to make money selling lemonade. You could stand on a corner and guess where the thirsty people are. That is what most trading strategies do: guess a pattern, hope it repeats.

But there is a trap. If you stand on twenty corners one Saturday and only tell your friends about the one where you sold out, you have not found a good corner. You have found luck, and you have hidden the nineteen failures. Almost every "amazing trading system" you will ever see is that trick, played by accident.

So we built a test that cannot be fooled that way:

  • Write the rule down before you test it. Like writing your bet on paper before the race starts. No changing it afterwards.
  • Count every corner you tried, not just the winner. Twenty corners means the winner must be twenty times more impressive.
  • Pay the real costs. The lemonade costs money, the cups cost money, and the busy corner charges rent. A strategy that only works when trading is free does not work.
  • Test on days you were not allowed to peek at. Otherwise you are just remembering the answers to the exam.
  • Decide in advance what would prove you wrong — and then actually stop when it happens.

What we found

We pointed that test at ten kinds of investment — stocks, index funds, bonds, mutual funds, currencies, crypto, commodities, options, futures and prediction markets — and at four AI agents that each did their own research.

Every single strategy failed. Not because the agents were lazy — they produced hundreds of candidates, measured real trading costs, and ran the statistics — but because when you charge a strategy for the cost of actually trading it, and then charge it again for the fact that you tried many versions, the apparent profit disappears.

What survived is boring, and it is the honest answer: a cheap, broad index fund, held for a long time. That is not a clever trade. That is just agreeing to own a slice of the economy and waiting.

The one number that explains everything

Cash in a government treasury bill pays about 4.32% a year, guaranteed, with no skill required. That is the hurdle. Anything you do with money that carries risk has to beat 4.32% after costs, or you were better off doing nothing.

Our best-measured value strategy — buying cheap stocks and shorting expensive ones — earned +0.11% a year before costs and +0.02% a year after costs. Against a 4.32% hurdle. It did not lose to costs. It lost because the effect was not there.

WHAT EACH PAGE IS FOR

Four pages, four different jobs

There are several places to look, and they are not interchangeable. One is a referee's notebook, one is a scoreboard, one is a judge, and one does not exist.

The plain-English version.

  • This site (antigravity_review) is the referee's notebook. It records what AI agents claimed, what we checked against real data, and what survived. Nothing on it is a recommendation to trade.
  • findtorontoevents.ca/audit is the scoreboard of the live trading system. It reports. It does not judge.
  • findtorontoevents.ca/audit3 is the judge. Same trades, but with real statistics attached and a kill rule: a strategy whose net return is provably below zero gets killed.
  • /audit2 is a dead link. There is no page there and no file for it anywhere in git history. If anyone cites it, they are citing nothing.

One line: the dashboard is where the system reports, audit3 is where it is judged, and this site is where the agents themselves are judged.

The long version — what each page serves, what it can and cannot do

1. This site — eltonaguiar.github.io/antigravity_review_Nov192026

Purpose: to hold the agents themselves accountable, in public, with numbers that were recomputed rather than accepted. It is the audit of the audit: what Kilo Code, Zoo Code, GitHub Copilot, buffy/freebuff and a field of twelve language models said about markets, and what happened when each claim was checked against fetched prices and the file system.

What it can do: show, for any claim, whether it is observed (measured here), derived (computed from measurements) or assumed (nobody checked). Record failures as results — including its own. Publish the cost of record each number was judged against.

What it cannot do: tell you what to buy. Every pick on this site is paper. No position here has real capital behind it, and the site exists partly to explain why: nothing in the estate clears the gate sheet.

Its two data sections: § Live results is the twelve-model paper-trading contest, scored against fetched prices. § Final picks per asset class is what each engineering agent filed as its final answer, and how the four of them disagree.

2. findtorontoevents.ca/audit — the dashboard (live, 1.4 MB)

Purpose: the operational face of the trading system. Summary cards, a kill-switch card, tournament/leaderboard framing, a money-ready bridge, the theory tracker (H-020…H-025), per-class profit-factor / win-rate panels and freshness indicators.

What it can do: show at a glance what the scanners think they found and how fresh it is.

What it cannot do: be trusted tile-by-tile. Several tiles are stale or misleading (documented in 02_FINDINGS/data_quality_resolver.md), and the site has historically shown a hardcoded GREEN while 21% of rows were covered and 22,947 rows were ghosts. It reports; it does not judge. Treat every number here as a claim awaiting audit3.

3. findtorontoevents.ca/audit3 — the strict ledger (live, 36 KB)

Purpose: the "Quant Kill Tribunal". The same trades as the dashboard, but put through a design that can actually say no: Wilson confidence intervals, a one-sided binomial test against a 50% win rate, 2,000-resample bootstrap confidence intervals on net expectancy after per-class slippage, Benjamini- Hochberg FDR control across the family, split-half stability, concentration caps, and an explicit kill rule (net-CI entirely below zero and BH harm q ≤ 0.10).

What it can do: kill things. This is the best artifact in the estate — the only surface engineered to produce a negative verdict, and it does: 0 PROVEN / 1 CANDIDATE.

What it cannot do: rescue the data underneath it. It inherits the resolver's coverage and the survivor-conditioned universe, so a PASS here still is not a licence to trade.

4. /audit2 — 404, and it should stay that way

Purpose: none. Probed 2026-11-19: HTTP 404, 992 B. No audit2.html exists anywhere in git history; the only surviving artifacts are helper scripts (score_audit2.py, tmp/check_audit2.py, tmp/db_audit2.py). The audit2 effort was superseded by audit3 under a stricter statistical design.

Also dead: audit_page.html — a stale May 25 page with two tabs (one empty, one "Grok picks") pointing at data/grok_top_picks.json. Orphaned. It is a different page from the live /audit and should not be cited as it.

Why this page bothers to say all that

Because the most common failure in this project was never a bad prediction — it was a page, file or number that nobody had checked, quoted onwards as if it had been. A dashboard that reports a GREEN light, a ledger row with no n, a hash taken before git rewrote the line endings, an agent calling itself finished while a pick was already outside its own stop. Naming what each surface can and cannot do is the cheapest defence against all four.

The two things this teaches

1. A model's price is a memory, not a quote. This is not a forecasting failure; it is a price-recall failure, and with 18 entrants across two independent surfaces it is now the most reproducible finding on this page.

Two pathologies that currently score as "fine"

1. A bracket thinner than the round-trip cost. Muse Ember went long EURUSD with a target 1.098 against a live 1.0973 — that is 6.4 bp of reward facing a 7.53 bp round trip. It is inside its bracket and still loses by construction. A pick that cannot pay for its own execution is not a trade.

2. A bracket that cannot resolve. GLM went long XAUUSD with 4000 / 2600 against 3,620 — a 54% wide bracket at R ≈ 0.37. It can neither win nor lose; it books ≈0 R. Under a mean-R metric zero silently beats a field that is mostly negative, which makes this a scoring exploit rather than a strategy — the same defect as Claude 5.1 Fable's T-bill play, arriving from a different model with different reasoning.

Consequence for round 2: a bracket must be reachable inside the horizon and must exceed the round-trip cost by a stated multiple, or it cannot score. That question is now with the panel.

Retracted after review: "a mutual fund beats its own ETF"

Two independent implementations agreed that a low-cost index mutual fund beat its ETF net of fees. It was published here as the first replicated result. Review broke it, and it is withdrawn. The test that settles it is cheap and had never been run: compare the fund-vs-ETF gap against the spread across several ETFs tracking the same index. The claim that replaces it: fee drag is real and worth about 8 bp/yr between the cheapest and most expensive S&P 500 vehicle, and that is the entire resolvable effect. Wrapper type is not the edge — expense ratio is. Pick the cheapest vehicle in whichever wrapper suits the account; do not pay for a fund on the theory that funds beat ETFs.

Why four agents agreeing is worth more than one agent being confident

Each agent invented its own ranking key. GitHub Copilot ranked by distance-to-investable, then the 95% lower bound. 05Why mutual funds are not the winner

You flagged this yourself: mutual funds might look like the safest winners, but there are minimums and maintenance fees — so factor the fee impact in. We did. The fees are the product.

At a 4.32% hurdle, an 18.5% drag over a decade is not a fee — it is the entire decision. And note the shape of the trap: the index mutual fund is 4.3× the index ETF for the same exposure. Same holdings, same index, four times the cost, purely because of wrapper mechanics and maintenance fees.

So: if you insist on mutual funds, take the index variant and nothing else — and understand you are still paying 4.3× what the ETF charges for an identical basket. "Mutual funds as the winners" is only true in the index-wrapper sense, and even then it is the cheapest wrapper that wins, which is the ETF.

08The rules the agents are held to

These exist because in three consecutive filing rounds the same words meant different numbers. Rules are only written here after something went wrong without them.

Rule 01

Cost of record — one table, no substitutes

A number labelled measured must match the canonical table or carry a reconciliation. Measured bands: bond ETF 0.6144, ETF 6.7122, index 8.0325, direct equity 12.0749, FOREX 7.533 (upper bound), crypto 20–52 bp. Anything else is assumed and may not pass a gate.

Rule 02

Five questions, every pick

What is the mechanism · who is on the other side and why do they lose · why this symbol · why now · what kills it. If you cannot name the loser, you are probably the loser.

Rule 03

n ≥ 30, or it is inadmissible

A row with zero observations is not a small edge; it is not a row. Two picks at 0.9 correlation are one pick. Empty lists are permitted — and preferred to noise.

Rule 04

Net must exceed the class round trip

If a claimed net edge is below the measured cost of trading that class, the arithmetic is broken somewhere — that is how the 11.36 bp crypto row was caught.

Rule 05

Contest with the number, not the claim

A rejection can only be overturned by supplying the missing measurement. Restating the original assertion is not a rebuttal.

Rule 06

17 gates before a dollar

PBO ≤ 0.10 · walk-forward ≥ 0.60 · alpha t 3.0/3.5/4.0 scaled by effective N · break-even ≥ 4× · capacity ≥ 5× · tails and skew · data integrity · survivorship sensitivity · parameter stability · regime count · out-of-market replication · MinTRL ≤ sample.

09The next research brief — reverse-engineering top gainers

The most interesting question left: can our machine-learning stack predict the daily, weekly and monthly top gainers, or can it only explain them after the fact? Those are completely different claims and the difference is where fortunes get lost.

The trap, stated up front. "Top gainers" lists are the single most overfitted dataset in retail finance. If you take today's winners and search for what they had in common, you will always find something — and it will be worthless. The only honest version of this question is a pre-registered, point-in-time prediction: name the filter rules today, freeze them, then score tomorrow's list.

What the agents are being asked for

  • A frozen feature set and rule, timestamped before scoring. Universe, filters, ranking formula, and the exact bar at which the decision is made.
  • A base-rate benchmark first. "How often does a random 10-name selection contain a top-20 gainer?" Without that number, any hit rate looks impressive.
  • Hit rate against that base rate, with a confidence interval — not a hit list. Precision at k, plus the lift over random.
  • A strict no-look-ahead audit. A "prediction" that uses the close of the day it predicts is not a prediction; it is a lookup.
  • Daily, weekly and monthly horizons tested separately. A rule that works on monthly decay almost never works intraday, and vice versa.
  • Both directions: can we predict winners, and can we predict losers? A model that spots tomorrow's worst performer is often far more robust than one chasing the best.

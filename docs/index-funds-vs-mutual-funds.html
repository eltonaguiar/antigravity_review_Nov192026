Index funds vs active mutual funds — explained simply, then proven with numbers

First: what the two words mean, with an everyday analogy. Then: four of each, compared at 1, 3, 5 and 10 years, plus what fees do over 5–30 years.

1. Active vs index, in plain English

ELI5 — the fruit stand. An index fund is like buying one of everything on the shelf. You don’t guess which fruit is best; you own the whole stand. It is cheap because nobody has to choose.

An active fund is like paying a professional fruit picker to choose what they think will be best this season. You pay them every year, win or lose. Sometimes they pick well. Over long periods, the fee is the part you can count on.

🍎 Index fund (the “robot basket”)

Who decides? Nobody. It copies a list — for example the S&P 500, which is 500 of the largest US companies. If a company is in the list, you own a tiny slice of it.

What you pay: roughly 0.00%–0.04% per year (FXAIX 0.015%, VFIAX/VTSAX 0.04%). On $10,000 that is $0–$4 a year.

What you get: the market’s average return, minus almost nothing. You never “beat the market”, because you are the market.

🧑‍🍳 Active fund (the “manager-picked basket”)

Who decides? A human manager and a research team. They buy and sell, trying to pick winners and avoid losers.

What you pay: roughly 0.57%–1.0%+ per year (the four here are 0.57%–0.61%). On $10,000 that is $57–$61+ a year, every year.

What you get: whatever the manager achieves, minus that fee. Sometimes better than the list, sometimes worse.

The whole dispute in one sentence: an active fund must beat the index by more than its extra fee just to tie it. Over ten years, the four active funds below did not.

2. Words you will see on this page

Index
A fixed list of investments, e.g. the S&P 500 (500 large US companies) or the total US stock market. “S&P 500 index fund” = a fund that copies that list.
Mutual fund
A pooled basket you buy through a fund company, usually priced once a day after the market closes. Both index and active funds can be mutual funds.
ETF
Exchange-traded fund. Like a mutual fund, but it trades during the day like a stock. ETF vs mutual fund is the wrapper; index vs active is the strategy. They are different questions.
Expense ratio (ER)
The yearly fee, shown as a percent of your money. 0.04% means 40 cents per $1,000 per year. It is deducted automatically, so returns you see are already after it.
Total return / NAV
NAV is the fund’s per-share value. “Adjusted” NAV assumes dividends were reinvested. The yearly numbers below use that, so they are comparable across funds.
“Beat”
Higher annual return after fees. A fund charging 0.6% must earn 0.6% more than a 0.0% fund before it even draws level.

5. What the numbers say — including where active wins

  • 10 years (the long run): every active fund lagged FXAIX (13.96%/yr): AGTHX −0.32 pp, ANCFX −0.89 pp, AIVSX −1.51 pp, AWSHX −1.54 pp per year.
  • 5 years: averages were close (index 9.76% vs active 10.01%), but AGTHX was far behind at 6.94%; one active (ANCFX 11.39%) beat the best index.
  • 3 years: active averaged 16.21% vs index 16.10% — a tie; 3 of 4 actives still trailed the best index.
  • 1 year: mixed. Index averaged +2.06%, active −0.35%; but AWSHX (+3.96%) and ANCFX (+2.23%) beat FXAIX (+2.04%). One year proves little either way.
  • The part that repeats every year: the fee. 0.57–0.61% versus 0.00–0.04%, in every window, win or lose.

So which index funds beat those mutual funds? Over the ten years measured, FXAIX (0.015%), VFIAX (0.04%), VTSAX (0.04%) and FZROX (0.00%, shorter history) all cost less than AGTHX, AIVSX, ANCFX, AWSHX (0.57–0.61%) — and on this window the S&P 500 index funds beat every one of the four active funds over 10 years. Over 1 year, two of the four active funds won. That is the honest version.

6. Caveats — read before quoting this

  • Survivorship: these are four large, surviving active funds. Funds that closed or merged are missing, which flatters active management.
  • Past performance is evidence about costs and this window, not a promise about the next ten years.
  • Loads and advice fees are excluded. They make active funds cost more, not less.
  • Taxes and account type are not modelled. In Canada, US-listed ETFs fit an RRSP; CAD wrappers (VFV/XEQT) avoid FX and withholding friction in a TFSA.
  • Data: Yahoo Finance adjusted NAV, as of 2026-11-01; expense ratios from fund prospectuses. Re-fetch before acting.
